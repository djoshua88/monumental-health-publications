The Five Domains: Where the Rating Actually Lives

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 2 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

The vocabulary the system uses

In the first installment, I gave you the map: the VA does not rate your diagnosis — it rates your function. The four-part process — claim, evidence, exam, decision — recurs at every stage, and the single regulation governing mental health ratings, 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, uses a ladder of percentages (0, 10, 30, 50, 70, 100) where each rung is described not by symptoms but by a level of occupational and social impairment.

Now I want to take you inside that language.

Here is what catches almost every veteran I have evaluated: the regulation is not asking how you feel. It is asking how your condition functions — how it shows up in your work, your relationships, your thinking, your daily routine, and your safety. And the words it uses are specific, learnable, and not at all the words most people use to describe their own experience.

That gap — between how a veteran feels and how the regulation asks the examiner to describe them — is where most under-documentation happens. This installment is about giving you the vocabulary to close it.

Five domains, one ladder

The § 4.130 formula is built around five domains of functional impairment. You will not find them listed as "the five domains" in the regulation itself — it weaves them into descriptive paragraphs at each rating tier — but they are what every examiner and rating specialist is actually assessing:

1. Occupational — your ability to work: maintain employment, perform tasks, meet deadlines, sustain productivity.

2. Social — your ability to maintain relationships: family, friendships, trust, communication, social engagement.

3. Cognitive — your thinking: memory, concentration, judgment, speech coherence, problem-solving.

4. Self-care — your daily living activities: hygiene, grooming, feeding yourself, maintaining a household.

5. Safety/Health — your ability to keep yourself safe and maintain your health: suicidal ideation, impulse control, danger to self or others, substance abuse consequences, medication adherence.

These are not abstractions. They are the axes along which your rating is constructed. When an examiner completes a Disability Benefits Questionnaire (DBQ), they are translating your life into these categories. When a rating specialist assigns a percentage, they are matching your functional level in each domain to the corresponding tier on the § 4.130 ladder.

The critical point: the rating is not an average across domains. It reflects the highest level of impairment present in any single domain. A veteran mildly impaired at work (30%) but experiencing suicidal ideation (70%) is not rated at some 50% midpoint. They are rated at 70%, because the safety/health domain alone meets that criterion.

This is not generosity. It is the law.

Each domain is assessed independently

The principle that each domain must be assessed separately — not blended into a composite impression — comes from Bankhead v. Shulkin, a 2017 decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. (The Bankhead court specifically addressed occupational and social impairment as distinct domains; the logic extends to all five functional domains.) The court held that occupational and social impairment are distinct domains requiring separate analysis. The logic extends to all five: a veteran can be profoundly impaired in one domain and minimally impaired in another, and the rating must reflect that, not smooth it into an average.

Consider a composite, hypothetical veteran — call him "M." — assembled from patterns I have seen across many evaluations. M. holds down a job. He shows up, meets deadlines, performs his tasks. By occupational functioning, he might look like a 30%. Socially, strained but not collapsed: some friction with family, reduced engagement, but relationships hold. Cognitively, mild forgetfulness under stress. Self-care is intact.

But M. has been having suicidal ideation. Not fleeting, not once. Recurring, several times a week, active enough to be frightening, though without a specific plan. He has not told anyone.

Under § 4.130, suicidal ideation appears at the 70% tier. The regulation's language is explicit: "suicidal ideation; impaired impulse control (such as unprovoked irritability with periods of violence)." M.'s safety/health impairment is at 70%. His occupational impairment is at 30%. Under Bankhead, these are assessed independently. Under Vazquez-Claudio v. Shinseki (Fed. Cir. 2013), the rating must reflect the actual level of impairment when all domains are considered. M. is not a 30% veteran who happens to feel bad. He is a 70% veteran whose occupational functioning happens to be preserved.

That distinction changes lives. And it depends entirely on whether the safety/health domain was assessed, documented, and given its own visibility.

Why Safety/Health deserves its own light

The fifth domain is the one most often invisible in the record.

The first four domains have analogues in the DBQ's checkbox structure. Safety/Health is different. The current DBQ form does not have a dedicated Safety/Health column in its checkbox section. Suicidality and impulse control appear in the regulation's rating criteria, but the form does not always prompt the examiner to document them with the specificity the rating requires.

This means safety/health findings — suicidal ideation, impulse control problems, danger to self or others, medication non-adherence with clinical consequences — are the findings most likely to be omitted or buried in a narrative paragraph that the rating specialist may not weight as heavily as a checked box.

That is a structural problem. It is compounded by the fact that safety/health is where veterans are most likely to minimize their own symptoms — out of shame, out of habit, out of the military-trained reflex to push through. A veteran who would never mention suicidal ideation in a brief exam is not being dishonest. They are being who they were trained to be. But the regulation does not compensate for training. It compensates for documented impairment.

Under Mauerhan v. Principi, 16 Vet. App. 436 (2002), the VA must consider all symptoms that affect impairment, not only those enumerated in the rating criteria. The regulation's language is a framework, not a ceiling. But the veteran — or their advocate — has to make sure those symptoms enter the record. The examiner cannot document what was never disclosed, and the rating specialist cannot weigh what was never documented.

The gap, and how to close it

Here is what I have seen again and again, on both sides of the exam table.

A veteran walks into a C&P exam and describes their life the way they live it: "I'm tired all the time. I can't focus. My wife and I fight. I don't see my friends anymore. Sometimes I think about whether it would be easier if I weren't here." That is honest, and it is real, and it is not the language the regulation uses.

The regulation asks: Is there suicidal ideation? What type — passive death wish, passive ideation, active without plan, active with plan? How often? When was the last time? Is there impaired impulse control? Is medication adherence inconsistent, and does that inconsistency lead to clinical deterioration?

The veteran said "sometimes I think about whether it would be easier if I weren't here." That is passive suicidal ideation. It maps to the 50% tier or higher. But unless someone — the veteran, the examiner, an advocate — names it in the regulation's terms, it may be documented as "depressed affect" and rated at 30% or 10%. Not because the veteran was wrong. Because the translation was never made.

Domain language is learnable. Using it in your own record — in a lay statement, in a buddy statement, in preparation for an exam — is not exaggeration. It is precision. You are not inventing symptoms. You are describing the ones you have in the vocabulary the system was built to hear.

Where the system structurally fails

I said in Installment 1 that the system was not designed with every veteran equally in mind. The safety/health domain is where that becomes most concrete.

It is the hardest domain to document and the easiest to miss. It requires the examiner to ask directly about suicidality, impulse control, and danger — and to document the answers with specificity. It requires the veteran to disclose things that are profoundly difficult to disclose, often to a stranger, in a time-limited exam. And it requires the cultural space to name suicidal ideation as a medical finding rather than a personal failing.

Veterans from communities where mental health is stigmatized — where seeking treatment is seen as weakness and suicidal ideation is something you do not say aloud — are systematically disadvantaged here. Not because their impairment is less severe. Because the disclosure path is structurally harder, and the form does not compensate. The DBQ has no Safety/Health checkbox that forces the question. The examiner has to ask. And the veteran has to answer.

Naming this is not an indictment. It is a map of where advocacy matters most. If you are a VSO preparing a veteran for an exam, the safety/health domain is where your preparation has the highest leverage. If you are a clinician completing a DBQ, Section 10 (Remarks) is where you document safety/health findings in the five-domain structure — even when the checkboxes do not prompt you to. If you are a veteran reading this, know that the questions about safety are not peripheral. They are where the rating actually lives.

What to take with you

1. The rating is built on five domains — Occupational, Social, Cognitive, Self-Care, Safety/Health — not a symptom inventory.

2. Each domain is assessed independently per Bankhead v. Shulkin. The rating reflects the highest level of impairment in any domain, not an average.

3. Safety/Health is a domain, not a footnote. Suicidal ideation, impulse control, and danger to self or others are rating-level findings that deserve their own visibility in the record.

4. The gap between how you feel and how the regulation describes you is closeable. Learning the domain language is not exaggeration — it is precision.

5. Where the system structurally fails, advocacy is the countermeasure. The safety/health domain is where documentation gaps cost veterans the most — and where a prepared veteran, a trained VSO, or a thorough clinician changes outcomes.

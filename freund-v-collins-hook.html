Primary Hook — "A Settlement Is Not a Resolution"

A settlement closes a case. It does not close the question the case raised.

On August 19, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims approved the settlement in Freund v. Collins (CAVC No. 21-4168), and the case was terminated. The CAVC precedential feed published confirmation of the approval on September 9, 2026.

The case concerned a VA computer system failure — known informally as the VACOLS glitch — that improperly closed, dismissed, or failed to process legacy disability appeals. Some of the affected claims dated back as far as 1990. Veterans who had filed appeals under the pre-Appeals Modernization Act framework and were entitled to adjudication instead received silence. Their appeals were not denied. They were not decided. They were lost inside a system that did not surface its own errors.

That is what the settlement resolves. A court-supervised remediation path now exists for veterans whose appeals were affected. VA has acknowledged the system failure. The litigation chapter has ended.

What remains unresolved is harder.

A software defect operated inside a federal adjudication system for years without detection. The veterans it affected did not know their appeals had been closed. The system did not flag its own mistakes. The question Freund raised is not whether this one glitch can be remediated — it is whether the architecture of veterans' benefits adjudication can detect its own failures before they become decades-old problems.

A settlement is accountability for what happened. It is not a guarantee that the conditions that allowed it to happen have changed.

For the veterans whose appeals now return to adjudication, the practical work begins: gathering current medical evidence, documenting the connection between a service-era condition and a present disability, and navigating a reopened claim that may span records older than the system that lost it.

The case is over. The obligation is not.

Supporting Hook 1 — The Systems Hook

When a government adjudication system fails, the people it serves are the last to know.

The Freund v. Collins settlement (approved August 19, 2026; CAVC No. 21-4168) resolves a class action over a VA software defect that closed or failed to process legacy disability appeals — some dating back to 1990. Veterans whose appeals were affected did not receive notice that their claims had been dismissed. The system did not surface its own error.

The settlement provides a remediation path. But the structural question persists: how does an adjudication system built to serve veterans verify that it has actually done the work it was asked to do?

A system that cannot detect its own failures is not fixed by a settlement. It is fixed by accountability mechanisms that operate before a class action becomes necessary.

Supporting Hook 2 — The Time Hook

Some of the disability appeals at the center of Freund v. Collins were filed in the 1990s.

A veteran who filed an appeal at age 30 may now be past 60. The condition they asked the VA to evaluate may have progressed. The service records that connected them to that condition may have been archived, transferred, or lost. The appeal itself was not denied — it was closed by a system error the veteran never knew about.

The settlement (approved August 19, 2026; CAVC No. 21-4168) gives these appeals a path back to adjudication. But reopening a claim after decades is not the same as adjudicating it on time. The veterans affected have already lived through the years their appeal spent in a system that lost it.

A settlement cannot return that time. It can only return the opportunity to be heard — and the obligation to make that opportunity meaningful now belongs to all of us.

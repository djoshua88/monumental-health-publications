The Record Speaks: Evidence, Lay Statements, and What Actually Proves a Claim

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 4 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

A claim is a body of evidence, not a single document

In the first three installments, I gave you the map and the vocabulary. You know the four moving parts — claim, evidence, exam, decision — and you know they repeat at every stage. You know the VA rates function, not diagnosis, under 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, and that the rating lives in five domains: Occupational, Social, Cognitive, Self-Care, and Safety/Health. You know the C&P exam is one room in the house, not the whole house — one evidence source among several.

Now I want to talk about what actually proves a claim. Not the exam. Not the diagnosis. The evidence — all of it, assembled.

Here is the thing that surprises veterans most: a rating specialist does not read one document and decide. They assemble a picture. They take your service treatment records, your post-service treatment records, your C&P exam report, your private DBQ if you have one, your own written statement, statements from people who knew you, prior rating decisions, and any medical opinions in the file — and they look at the whole of it. The claim is not a single piece of paper. It is a body of evidence, and the rating specialist's job is to see what that body, taken together, proves.

This matters because veterans often come to the process believing that one document will carry the day — the DD-214, the C&P exam, a single therapy note. Each of those is real and important. But none of them, alone, is the case. The case is what the evidence proves when it is read as a whole. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.159, the VA has a duty to assist in developing that body of evidence — obtaining records, scheduling exams. But the duty to assist is not a duty to build your case for you. The evidence you bring, and the evidence you help create, is what closes the gap.

What the record is made of

Let me name the layers, because knowing what each layer does — and what it cannot do — is where most veterans gain leverage.

Service treatment records and personnel files establish the in-service event: the thing that happened in service that you are now saying is connected to your current condition. Sick-call visits, mental health contacts, disciplinary actions, performance evaluations, deployment records. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303, direct service connection requires a current diagnosis, an in-service event, and a medical nexus. Service records are where the second element lives. Post-service treatment records establish the current condition and its longitudinal course. If you received mental health care after discharge — through the VA, community providers, or private clinicians — those records show the condition exists, has persisted, and has functional consequences. They are powerful because they are contemporaneous, written by clinicians treating you, not constructing a case. But they are incomplete in a specific way: treating clinicians document for care, not for the rating formula. They note symptoms and medication adjustments; they rarely use the five-domain language from § 4.130. The C&P exam report — which we covered in Installment 3 — is the VA's structured assessment of your condition against the rating criteria. It is one evidence source among several. A well-documented exam is valuable. A thinly documented exam is a common, correctable problem — but it is not the whole case. Private DBQs and medical opinions — including independent medical opinions that supply nexus — are evidence the veteran can commission. We will cover nexus and IMOs in depth in Installment 5; for now, know that they exist and that they carry weight when they are well-reasoned. Lay evidence — your own statement, buddy statements, family statements — is the layer most veterans underestimate and the one this installment is most about.

Lay evidence is competent evidence

Here is a legal point that changes how veterans understand their own role in the process.

Lay evidence — the written statement of a person who is not a medical professional — is competent evidence for things a layperson can personally observe. Symptoms. Behavior changes. Functional decline. The timing of onset. What the veteran was like before and what they are like now. Whether they sleep. Whether they isolate. Whether they can hold a job. Whether they erupt. Whether they have said things that frightened the people who love them.

What lay evidence cannot do is opine on medical causation — the nexus question, whether the condition was caused by service. That line was drawn by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit in Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004). The court held that establishing the causal link between a current disability and in-service events generally requires medical evidence. Lay evidence can describe what a person observed; it cannot substitute for a medical conclusion about why a condition exists.

But here is the critical nuance: in mental health claims, the rating is about function, not causation. The five domains — Occupational, Social, Cognitive, Self-Care, Safety/Health — are things a layperson can witness. A spouse can describe the veteran who stopped sleeping through the night. A fellow service member can describe the change they saw after a specific deployment. A supervisor can describe the decline in reliability and concentration. A friend can describe the social withdrawal. These are not medical conclusions. They are observations, and they map directly onto the regulatory language the rating specialist applies.

The Federal Circuit has also recognized that lay evidence can be competent to establish a diagnosis when a layperson is competent to identify the condition, when they are reporting a contemporaneous medical diagnosis, or when lay testimony describing symptoms supports a later diagnosis. But for our purposes, the key point is simpler: you do not need a medical degree to be a witness to impairment. You need to have been there.

The people who see you every day

This is why buddy and family statements are, in my experience, often the strongest functional evidence available in a mental health claim.

The VA rates what the condition does to a veteran's functioning — not how the veteran describes their suffering in a forty-five-minute exam. And the people who see the veteran every day — the partner who watches them not sleep, the adult child who watches them withdraw, the friend who watched them change after deployment, the fellow service member who was there when it happened — are the people who witness the day-to-day impairment the regulation actually rates.

Consider a composite, hypothetical veteran — call her "R." — assembled from patterns I have seen across many evaluations. R. was social before service. She hosted, she called, she showed up. After her second deployment, she stopped. She screens calls. She has not seen friends in over a year. She leaves her home only for work and essential errands. She sleeps poorly and says so to her partner. She has episodes of irritability that frighten her teenage son. She has not sought mental health treatment because she was raised to handle things herself and because the nearest provider is forty minutes away.

R.'s C&P exam, if she has one, will capture some of this. But R.'s partner — who has watched the withdrawal, the sleep disruption, the irritability, the isolation — can write a statement that documents the functional decline in the exact domains the regulation rates: Social (withdrawal from relationships), Safety/Health (irritability that frightens her son), Cognitive (difficulty concentrating that the partner observes), Self-care (neglect of household routines). That statement is lay evidence. It is competent for what the partner personally observed. It cannot diagnose or opine on causation. But it can witness — and in a mental health claim, witnessing function is the core of the case.

The same is true for buddy statements from service. A fellow service member who saw the in-service event, or who observed the behavioral change during or after service, can establish the in-service element when service treatment records are thin or absent. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(b), the veteran is presumed to have been in sound condition at service entry unless the VA can show clear and unmistakable evidence to the contrary. Buddy statements can establish what happened after that presumption — the event, the change, the onset.

The veteran's own statement is evidence — and how you write it matters

Your own written statement — sometimes called a lay statement or statement in support of claim — is evidence. This is a point many veterans miss. They believe their own words do not count unless a clinician writes them. They do. For everything short of the medical nexus conclusion, your own statement carries weight.

But how you write it materially affects that weight.

In Installment 2, I gave you the five domains and the regulatory vocabulary that maps to them. Here is where that vocabulary earns its keep. A statement that says "I'm tired all the time, I can't focus, my wife and I fight, I don't see my friends anymore, sometimes I think about whether it would be easier if I weren't here" is honest and real — and it is not the language the regulation uses. A statement that says the same things in domain-specific terms — occupational impairment (difficulty sustaining productivity, missing deadlines), social impairment (withdrawal from relationships, reduced engagement), cognitive impairment (difficulty concentrating, memory problems), safety/health impairment (passive suicidal ideation, several times per week) — is the same veteran, the same life, described in the vocabulary the rating specialist is applying. That is not exaggeration. It is precision. You are not inventing symptoms. You are describing the ones you have in the language the system was built to hear.

This is the single most actionable thing a veteran can do for their own claim: write the statement in functional, domain-specific terms. If you are a VSO helping a veteran, this is where your preparation has the highest leverage. If you are a family member, you can help by being specific — not "he's different now" but "he used to call his sister every week and has not in fourteen months; he stopped attending his weekly basketball game; he has not slept through the night since he came home."

Continuity, gaps, and why they matter

Now I want to talk about something that is not about any single document but about the shape of the record over time: continuity.

The VA's evaluation process assumes a documented treatment history that traces a condition from service to the present. It assumes that if a condition existed in service and exists now, there is a paper trail connecting the two. When that trail is continuous, the claim is relatively straightforward. When it has gaps, the claim gets harder — and when the gaps are unexplained, the claim often gets under-rated.

A gap is not fatal. A veteran who did not receive treatment for a period — because they lacked insurance, because they were between providers, because they were managing on their own, because they were ashamed to seek care — can still establish that the condition persisted through the gap. But the gap has to be addressed. An unexplained gap invites the rating specialist to infer that the condition was absent during the period with no documentation, which can pull the effective date down, reduce the rating, or both. An explained gap — "I did not seek treatment from 2015 to 2019 because I lost my insurance and could not afford private care; the condition persisted throughout, as my family can attest" — is a different evidentiary picture. The same gap, explained, does not disappear, but it stops silently eroding the claim.

This is where continuity becomes an equity issue, and I want to be direct about it.

Veterans without stable access to care are systematically disadvantaged here. If you could not afford mental health treatment for years after discharge, your post-service record will show a gap — not because the condition resolved, but because the documentation infrastructure was absent. If you lived in a provider desert — rural communities, underserved urban areas, communities where the nearest mental health professional is an hour or more away — your record will be thinner than a veteran who had continuous access to care. If you were raised in a community where seeking mental health treatment was culturally punished, you may never have sought care during or after service, and your record will reflect that — not the absence of the condition, but the absence of the disclosure path.

These are not individual failings. They are structural features of a process that assumes a particular kind of veteran with a particular kind of paper trail. And they are compounded by a historical catastrophe that many veterans do not know about.

The 1973 NPRC fire: when the record itself was destroyed

On July 12, 1973, a fire at the National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) in St. Louis, Missouri, destroyed approximately 16–18 million Official Military Personnel Files. The fire burned for days. Among the losses were an estimated 80% of Army personnel records for veterans discharged between 1912 and 1960, and a significant portion of Air Force records for veterans discharged between 1947 and 1964. For the veterans whose files were destroyed — and their survivors — the backbone of any direct service connection claim simply ceased to exist. Not because the veteran failed to document their service, but because the government lost the documentation.

The VA is required to work around this. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.159, the VA has a duty to assist claimants in obtaining evidence, and when official records are unavailable, the VA is supposed to accept alternative evidence — reconstructed records, secondary sources, and lay evidence, including buddy statements. The National Archives has established processes for reconstructing what can be reconstructed from auxiliary sources. But the veteran has to know that alternative evidence is allowed, that buddy statements can substitute for missing service records, and that the duty to assist applies even when the records were lost by the government, not the veteran.

This is not a historical footnote. The veterans affected by the 1973 fire — and their survivors filing derivative claims — are still navigating this gap. And the pattern it represents — a documentation gap that is not the veteran's fault but that the veteran must work to overcome — is the same pattern that access-to-care gaps, MST-related underreporting, and mental health stigma create in different ways. The system assumes a record exists. When it does not, the burden of producing alternative evidence falls on the veteran. Naming that burden is not a complaint. It is the first step in discharging it.

What to take with you

1. A claim is a body of evidence, not a single document. The rating specialist assembles a picture from multiple sources — service records, post-service treatment, the C&P exam, lay statements, medical opinions. No one document is the case; the case is what the evidence proves when read as a whole.

2. Lay evidence is competent evidence for what a layperson can observe. Symptoms, behavior, functional changes, timing — these are things you can witness. What lay evidence cannot do is opine on medical causation; Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004), draws that line. But in mental health claims, where the rating is about function, most of what matters is observable by laypeople.

3. Buddy and family statements are often the strongest functional evidence available. The people who see you every day witness the day-to-day impairment the regulation actually rates. They are your most credible witnesses for the five domains.

4. Continuity matters, and an explained gap is different from an unexplained one. A gap in treatment is not fatal, but an unexplained gap is a common reason for under-rating. Name the reason. And understand that continuity is an equity issue — veterans without stable access to care are systematically disadvantaged, and the 1973 NPRC fire destroyed the records of millions of veterans through no fault of their own.

5. Your own statement is evidence — and writing it in functional, domain-specific terms materially increases its weight. You are already a witness to your own life. Using the five-domain vocabulary from Installment 2 is not exaggeration. It is precision. It is the single most actionable thing you can do for your own claim.

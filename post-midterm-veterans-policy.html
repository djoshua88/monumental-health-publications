What the 2026 Election Means for Veterans Healthcare, AI Regulation, and Telehealth Policy

A policy analysis of what changes — and what doesn't — when congressional power shifts.

On November 3, 2026, American voters delivered a decisive verdict. The Democratic Party won control of both chambers of Congress — 254 seats in the House and a Senate majority — in what analysts called a fundamental realignment driven by economic anxiety, foreign policy frustration, and concerns about democratic backsliding (AP, NBC News, November 4, 2026). New committee leadership will reopen legislative debates that stalled under unified Republican control and reshape the policy landscape for veterans healthcare, clinical AI governance, and telehealth access.

This is not a partisan argument. It is a policy analysis of what changes — and what doesn't — when congressional power shifts.

1. VA Appropriations: Stability, With Pressure to Expand

The VA operates on the largest healthcare budget in the federal government — over $120 billion annually in medical care. Under Republican-led Congress, VA funding held roughly steady in nominal terms, though inflation-adjusted purchasing power eroded and workforce shortages persist across clinical specialties (VA OIG, GAO, 2025-2026).

A Democratic majority does not automatically mean spending increases. Appropriations still require bicameral negotiation and presidential signature. But the House Appropriations Military Construction-VA Subcommittee gavel would pass to the ranking Democratic member. Historically, Democratic appropriators have prioritized maintaining or expanding VA medical care accounts and resisted proposals that offset new veterans' spending by cutting existing benefits — a tension that surfaced directly in the TCAVA debate.

The practical implication: VA healthcare funding is likely stable or growing modestly. The risk of continuing-resolution-driven hiring freezes — which have cost the VA clinical staffing windows in past cycles — may diminish. The real question is whether new funding reaches the front lines: staffing, rural access, and mental health infrastructure.

2. AI Regulation: From Preemption to Federal Safety Framework

The 2026 AI policy landscape has been defined by federal absence. President Trump revoked Executive Order 14110 — the Biden-era AI safety framework — in January 2025. In December 2025, he signed an executive order seeking to preempt state AI laws and establish a single national framework that critics argued prioritized industry speed over safety (White House statements, December 2025).

States filled the vacuum. California Governor Newsom signed executive orders accelerating AI safety laws, including "kill switch" requirements endorsed by Anthropic and OpenAI (September 2026). California SB 574 regulated lawyers' use of agentic AI, requiring personal verification of cited material (October 2026).

A Democratic Congress changes this equation. The House Energy and Commerce Committee and the Senate HELP Committee — both likely to see new Democratic chairs — have jurisdiction over AI safety legislation. Democratic leadership has signaled interest in federal frameworks including transparency requirements, pre-deployment testing for high-risk systems, and protections against algorithmic discrimination.

For veterans healthcare, this matters directly. The VA is one of the largest deployers of clinical AI in the United States — from the REACH VET suicide risk prediction model to ambient AI scribe pilots to imaging algorithms. A federal AI safety framework with clinical AI provisions would establish guardrails the VA currently navigates through internal policy alone. Our prior analysis of the STORM opioid risk stratification tool and REACH VET 2.0 underscores why external oversight matters: clinical AI tools with demonstrated mortality reductions still require ongoing bias auditing and transparency about what the algorithm is actually doing.

Passage depends on whether Democratic leadership prioritizes AI legislation early in the 120th Congress — and whether a Trump White House signs or vetoes it.

3. TCAVA: A More Receptive Floor Environment, but the Same Structural Tension

The Take Care of America's Veterans Act (TCAVA, HR 9237 / S 4744) packages 62+ bipartisan bills, including the Major Richard Star Act, which would expand combat-related disability compensation. The bill's most controversial provision — Section 108 — would eliminate compensable tinnitus ratings for approximately 1.5 million veterans and reduce sleep apnea ratings, redirecting an estimated $57-60 billion to fund other provisions (Congress.gov, GovTrack, July 2026; policy-legislative-analyst briefing, July 6, 2026).

The discharge petition for the standalone Major Richard Star Act (HR 2102) reached 218 signatures on September 15, 2026, crossing the threshold to force a floor vote (clerk.house.gov). As of this writing, that vote has not yet occurred. A Democratic-majority House may offer a more receptive floor environment — Democratic leadership has historically supported expanding combat-related disability benefits, and 47 Senate Democrats signed a Blumenthal/King letter opposing Section 108's benefit cuts.

But the structural tension remains. TCAVA's design — funding new benefits by cutting existing ones — represents a zero-sum approach that has drawn opposition from the VFW, DAV, and IAVA, with only qualified support from the American Legion. A new Congress could separate the popular Star Act from Section 108, or revisit TCAVA's packaging entirely.

The key signal is whether the 120th Congress treats veterans' benefits as a budget to be balanced internally or as an obligation to be funded on its own terms. That is a policy philosophy question, not a partisan one — but the election results make the latter approach more likely.

4. Veterans Mental Health: Committee Leadership Changes

The House Committee on Veterans' Affairs (HVAC) is currently chaired by Representative Mike Bost (R-IL), with Representative Mark Takano (D-CA) as Ranking Member. The Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs (SVAC) is chaired by Senator Jerry Moran (R-KS), with Senator Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) as Ranking Member (clerk.house.gov; senate.gov, 119th Congress).

Under a Democratic majority, Takano would likely assume the HVAC chair and Blumenthal the SVAC chair. Both have track records on veterans' mental health. Takano previously chaired HVAC during the 117th Congress and oversaw passage of the Honoring our PACT Act, prioritizing mental health staffing and suicide prevention. Blumenthal has been active on Veterans Crisis Line oversight and was a lead signatory opposing TCAVA's Section 108 benefit cuts.

Committee leadership matters because it sets the hearing agenda, controls oversight investigations, and determines which bills get markup. Expect renewed oversight hearings on VA mental health staffing, the Veterans Crisis Line, and implementation of 988's veteran routing — issues we have analyzed in our coverage of 988 and REACH VET.

5. Telehealth: The December 31 Cliff and the Case for Permanence

The DEA and HHS issued a fourth temporary extension of COVID-19 telemedicine flexibilities on December 31, 2025, extending the ability to prescribe controlled substances via telemedicine through December 31, 2026 (Federal Register, December 31, 2025). Without further action, those flexibilities expire — returning to the Ryan Haight Act's in-person evaluation requirement for controlled substance prescribing.

The VA received a permanent telemedicine rulemaking in January 2025, authorizing VA practitioners to prescribe controlled substances via telemedicine within the scope of their VA practice (Federal Register, January 17, 2025). This provides insulation for VA-operated telehealth, but the broader flexibilities — affecting community providers, rural health clinics, and non-VA specialists who see veterans through community care referrals — remain temporary.

A Democratic Congress may pursue permanent telehealth legislation codifying these flexibilities beyond December 31. The coalition for telehealth permanence is unusually bipartisan — rural state senators from both parties have consistently supported flexibilities, and the COVID-era precedent demonstrated that remote prescribing can be done safely with appropriate safeguards. The question is whether a permanent framework clears both chambers and the White House before the deadline, or becomes a 120th Congress priority.

For rural veterans — the 4.4 million veterans living outside metropolitan areas — the stakes are concrete. A veteran receiving medication-assisted treatment for opioid use disorder via telemedicine, or a veteran in a rural community care arrangement seeing a psychiatrist remotely, faces disrupted care if the flexibilities lapse. The VA's own telehealth infrastructure is robust, but community care providers operating under DEA registration depend on the broader flexibilities.

What Doesn't Change

Election results change committee gavels and floor schedules. They do not change the underlying needs:

  • The veteran suicide rate remains approximately 17.5 per day (VA 2025 National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report).
  • VA wait times increased at 71% of 21 VA Medical Centers surveyed, with neurology averaging 76.3 days (VVF report, April 2026).
  • Clinical AI tools like REACH VET and STORM require ongoing oversight regardless of which party holds the gavel.
  • Rural broadband gaps still gate telehealth access for millions of veterans.

Policy is the machinery. The mission — ensuring that veterans receive the care, benefits, and dignity they earned — is the work. How effectively a new Congress serves veterans will depend on whether stakeholders hold the system accountable to outcomes, not optics.

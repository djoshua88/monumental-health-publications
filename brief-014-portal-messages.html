The Inbox Is a Clinical Space: What AI-Drafted Messages Must Get Right for Veterans

When a health system changes how people reach their care teams, it should also define what trustworthy digital communication will require next.

On September 30, 2026, the legacy My HealtheVet page began redirecting veterans to My HealtheVet on VA.gov, where they can manage health records, medications, appointments, and messages.1 That transition is a platform change—not evidence that the Department of Veterans Affairs uses artificial intelligence to draft replies to veterans. But it opened a timely design window. When a health system changes how people reach their care teams, it should also define what trustworthy digital communication will require next. The transition has now been live for 26 days.

Across health care, some systems have begun piloting or implementing AI-generated drafts in clinical inbox workflows.3 The appeal is understandable. A tool that prepares a first draft might reduce some of the cognitive strain created by a growing message queue. Yet speed is not the only value at stake. A portal reply can address a scheduling question, but it can also contain a symptom update, a concern about a medication, or a sign that someone needs more immediate support. The inbox is not merely an administrative channel. It is a clinical space.

For veteran-facing communication, the right question is therefore not, "Can AI write a plausible reply?" It is, "What must a care system do before any AI-assisted reply can be trusted?" Current evidence offers useful design signals, but it does not establish veteran- or VHA-specific outcomes. The standard should begin with that boundary and with one nonnegotiable principle: AI may draft; an accountable person must decide.

Patient acceptance is conditional—not blanket permission

Published July 7, 2026, a qualitative study based on one-time interviews with 40 adult patients from a single academic health system found that participants were generally comfortable with AI-drafted portal messages when clinicians reviewed, approved, and remained accountable for them.2 Participants broadly endorsed disclosure, while preferences for tone, length, detail, empathy, and disclosure format varied by context.2

Those findings matter, but they are not a population-wide approval rating. The study used vignette prompts, involved a small purposively sampled group, and was not expected to generalize to all patients or settings.2 It did not measure real-world portal behavior, and it did not study veterans or a VHA workflow.

A separate 2025 Duke survey adds another signal, not a mandate. Of 2,511 invited patient-advisory-committee members, 1,455 responded, a 57.9% response rate.5 In randomized hypothetical scenarios, AI-authored response text had a small satisfaction advantage over human-authored text—0.30 points on a five-point scale. Satisfaction was slightly lower when the disclosure identified AI involvement than when it identified a human author, by 0.13 points, or provided no disclosure, by 0.09 points.5 The authors still recommended simple disclosure to uphold autonomy and reduce information imbalance.5

That result should not be reframed as evidence that people prefer nondisclosure. The main experiment inferred the effect of disclosure through satisfaction ratings; it did not directly ask whether nondisclosure should be policy. The single-system, hypothetical-vignette design and a respondent group skewed older, more educated, non-Hispanic White, and retired also limit generalizability.5

Together, these studies suggest that people may accept AI assistance when accountability and transparency remain visible. They do not show that veterans hold the same preferences, that one disclosure format will work for every message, or that acceptance establishes safety.

Perceived workload is not the same as measured time

The case for AI-drafted replies often begins with clinician burden. Here, too, the distinctions matter.

In a five-week, prospective, single-group Stanford Health Care quality-improvement study, 162 clinicians had a mean per-clinician AI-draft utilization rate of about 20%.3 Among the paired survey respondents—73 for task load and 71 for work exhaustion—task-load and work-exhaustion scores decreased. The implementation was associated with those reported reductions; it was not a controlled trial that established AI as the cause.3

Meanwhile, none of four EHR-derived measures—other-action, reply-action, reading, and writing time—changed significantly.3 In other words, participants in the paired survey subsets reported a lighter burden, but the measured time outcomes did not demonstrate faster message handling.

That is not a contradiction. A draft may change how demanding a task feels without reducing the seconds recorded in the EHR. But the five-week design, single group, one institution, two departments, four clinician types, possible novelty bias, and possible Hawthorne effect limit causal inference and generalizability.3 The study offers a promising workflow signal, not proof of durable efficiency, reduced burnout, or benefit in VHA settings.

"Clinician reviewed" is not a safety result

Human review is essential, but a review checkbox is not enough. The operational question is whether the reviewer reliably notices and corrects what matters.

A 2025 cross-sectional simulation asked 20 practicing primary care physicians from 13 Baltimore–Washington-area sites to respond to 18 simulated portal-message exchanges with AI drafts available. Four drafts contained prespecified errors.4 Depending on the error, 13 to 15 clinicians insufficiently addressed it, and 35% to 45% of the erroneous drafts were submitted completely unedited.4

The result demonstrates automation-deference risk under the tested simulation: a fluent draft can invite trust even when it contains an inaccuracy or a potentially harmful omission. It does not estimate how often deployed systems generate errors, how often clinicians miss errors in routine practice, or how often a patient is harmed.4 Participants lacked full patient records, worked remotely without supervision, and were not compared with a no-AI group.4

This is the difference between simulation risk and demonstrated safety. A simulation can reveal a credible failure mode. It cannot, by itself, establish the error rate of a deployed product. Likewise, patient comfort cannot establish clinical safety, and perceived workload relief cannot establish time savings. Responsible policy must keep these evidence categories separate.

A veteran-first standard for augmentation

None of the four studies above evaluated a veteran-only sample, a veteran subgroup, a VHA clinical setting, or a VHA workflow.2345 They should inform design questions, not support claims about what veterans prefer or how VHA clinicians will perform. A higher standard for veteran-facing messaging is a normative commitment to safety, dignity, access, and trust—not a study finding that these sources can prove.

That commitment can be translated into five requirements.

1. Accountable human sign-off. Every AI-assisted patient-facing message should have a named, authorized human reviewer who is responsible for its final content. Approval must mean an active clinical judgment—not passive acceptance of polished language. Systems should preserve who drafted, who edited, who approved, and what changed.

2. Risk-tiered limits. Administrative and low-risk messages should not be governed like messages involving worsening symptoms, mental-health concerns, medication effects, diagnostic uncertainty, or urgent access needs. Higher-risk categories need stricter review, clearer routing, and, where safe oversight cannot be assured, no AI-generated draft at all. Classification rules should be tested against real failure modes rather than treated as static labels.

3. Standardized disclosure. People should not have to decode vague phrases such as "technology assisted." A brief, plain-language statement should explain when AI helped prepare a message and affirm that a member of the care team reviewed and approved it. The wording and placement should be tested for comprehension and accessibility, while allowing more context when the stakes are higher.

4. Continuous audits. Health systems should measure accuracy, clinically meaningful omissions, edit rates, unedited-send rates, overrides, escalation behavior, and recurring failure patterns. Audits should also examine whether performance or editing burdens differ across language, disability, race, ethnicity, age, gender, geography, or other relevant groups. Averages can hide inequitable failure. Monitoring should lead to retraining, workflow changes, tighter limits, or suspension when thresholds are missed.

5. Visible urgent and crisis escalation. Portal messages should plainly state that the inbox is not an emergency service and may not be read immediately. Every relevant interface and reply should direct people with urgent or crisis needs to immediate, human help through the health system's approved escalation pathways. AI should never be the final gatekeeper for a message suggesting imminent danger or rapidly worsening risk.

The promise must be larger than the product

The strongest case for AI in the inbox is not that it can sound human. It is that, under disciplined limits, it may help people communicate more clearly while keeping care teams accountable. That promise fails if organizations confuse patient satisfaction with consent, reported burden with measured time, review with error detection, or simulation findings with real-world harm rates.

The September 30 My HealtheVet transition did not announce AI-drafted VA replies. It did, however, remind us that digital infrastructure shapes the relationship between veterans and the people responsible for their care. The transition is now live. Before AI writes any part of that relationship, the rules should be clear: disclose the assistance, limit it by risk, audit what happens, preserve human escalation, and make a person—not an algorithm—answerable for every clinical message.

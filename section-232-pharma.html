Six Layers of Duty: What the New Pharma Tariff Regime Means for Veterans' Drug Supply

Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs have been active law since July 31 — and a single import can now carry up to six layers of duty. Here's what that means for the 9 million veterans who rely on VA prescriptions.

Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs have been active law since July 31, 2026. The initial shock has passed; what remains is the compounding, structural impact on drug supply chains. A single pharmaceutical import can now carry up to six separate layers of duty. Meanwhile, Section 338 adds a 50% tariff on 554 Canadian tariff lines effective August 19, and the full 232 regime extends to all remaining importers on September 29.

This is not a single-tariff story. It is a compound duty story — and for the millions of veterans who rely on the VA for their medications, it is not abstract policy. It is personal.

The Sextuple Tariff Stack: How Many Layers Can One Import Carry?

Here is what the full duty stack looks like today:

1. MFN duty — The baseline, ranging from 0% to 38% by HTS classification. Historically zero for most finished drugs.

2. Section 232 — The new pharmaceutical-specific tariff. Default: 100% ad valorem on patented drugs, APIs, and key starting materials. The reduced tiers are structured by compliance type and country of origin, not as a menu: 0% for companies holding both an onshoring agreement AND an MFN pricing agreement (through January 20, 2029); 10% for UK-origin products; 15% for EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, and Liechtenstein products; 20% for companies with a Commerce-approved onshoring plan but no MFN pricing agreement (escalating to 100% after April 2, 2030). Any importer without an applicable agreement or country designation faces the full 100% rate.

3. Section 301 — Tariffs on Chinese-origin goods: 7.5% to 25%, depending on the list.

4. Section 122 — A 15% ad valorem surcharge on most imports (suppressed where Section 232 applies on the same HTS line, but still in effect on uncovered goods).

5. Anti-dumping and countervailing duties (ADCVD) — Per-order duties that can exceed 100%.

6. MPF and HMF — Merchandise processing fee and harbor maintenance fee: ~0.47% combined on all formal entries.

For a Chinese-origin patented drug without onshoring or MFN coverage, the theoretical stack is staggering: MFN (up to 6%) + Section 301 (7.5–25%) + Section 232 (100%) + Section 122 (15%, where not suppressed) + ADCVD (variable) + fees (~0.47%). Anti-stacking rules help — Section 232 suppresses Section 122 on the same HTS line — but the effective duty on many pharmaceutical imports remains unprecedented.

Veterans' Prescription Drug Costs: Where the Stack Hits the Supply Chain

The VA procures approximately $14 billion in pharmaceuticals annually. Roughly 53% of patented drugs distributed domestically are imported; only 15% of APIs by volume are domestically sourced — a figure cited in the Section 232 proclamation itself as a national security concern.

Generics are exempt from Section 232 — for now. The proclamation directs Commerce to review within one year whether to extend tariffs to generics. That "at this time" qualifier is a ticking clock, not a permanent reprieve.

For veterans, the impact works through the supply chain. VA copayment rates for prescription medications are modest — $5 per 30-day supply for Tier 1 (preferred generics) and $11 for Tier 3 (brand-name) for Priority Groups 2–8. Priority Group 1 veterans pay no medication copay. But these copayments apply only to drugs on the VA formulary. Non-formulary drugs — many patented, many imported — cost the veteran the full price.

Section 232 tariffs apply at the border, before any VA price negotiation. Importers — including VA wholesale distributors — pay the duty, and those costs pass through. A 100% tariff on a branded drug not yet on formulary delays formulary addition and raises the cost ceiling. Veterans with service-connected disabilities in Priority Groups 1–5 pay no medication copay, but veterans in Priority Groups 7 and 8 — those without compensable service-connected conditions and with higher incomes — face the most acute version of this pressure: they pay the highest copays and are least able to absorb price increases on non-formulary medications.

The American Action Forum estimated that a 100% worldwide tariff on APIs used in domestically produced generics could raise average finished-drug prices by approximately 30% — a modeling projection from April 2026, predating the July 31 implementation, not an observed outcome. The direction is unambiguous: up.

Section 338: A New Front Opens August 19

As if the Section 232 regime were not enough, another tariff layer arrives on August 19, 2026, when Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 imposes an additional 50% ad valorem duty on 554 Canadian tariff lines.

Section 338 has never before been used in the modern era. Three presidential proclamations signed on July 20 target alcohol, dairy, and a broad basket of goods under the heading of "motor vehicles" — a heading that is misleading, because the actual motor vehicles are already covered by Section 232 and excluded from 338. The largest proclamation sweeps across Chapters 84 and 85 of the tariff schedule: machinery, electrical equipment, and industrial inputs. That includes equipment used in pharmaceutical manufacturing and cold-chain logistics.

Two features make Section 338 particularly consequential for pharma supply chains. First, USMCA does not exempt covered goods. A valid certificate of origin provides zero relief. Second, Section 338 carries no fixed expiry — it remains in force indefinitely unless the President modifies or terminates it. Canada is a significant source of pharmaceutical-grade industrial inputs and cold-chain equipment. Any HTS code in Chapters 84 or 85 sourced from Canada now requires an immediate landed-cost reassessment. Importantly, Section 338 does not directly tax Canadian-origin pharmaceutical products — Chapter 30 (medicaments) is not among the covered tariff lines. Its impact on drug supply chains is indirect: higher costs for manufacturing equipment, industrial inputs, and cold-chain infrastructure.

The Onshoring Illusion: Timelines Don't Match the Urgency

The administration cites approximately $400 billion in promised U.S. pharmaceutical manufacturing investment as evidence that Section 232 is working. Eli Lilly has announced a $27 billion domestic manufacturing expansion. Pfizer's Kalamazoo sterile injectables facility — the flagship onshoring example — is not expected operational until 2029. Teva's generic API onshoring agreement has no confirmed timeline.

The central tension: tariffs are collectible today, but the manufacturing capacity to replace imported supply is 2 to 5 years away. The tiered structure creates a policy catch-22. Tariffs punish importers for supply chain decisions they cannot unilaterally change — onshoring requires permitting, regulatory approval, labor, and infrastructure measured in years. Meanwhile, the 100% default rate is not a future threat. It is a present cost, paid at every customs entry by companies without agreements.

In the gap between tariff effective dates and operational onshoring, something has to give. For the supply chain, that "something" is higher landed costs that flow through to payers — including the VA, Medicare, Medicaid, and ultimately patients. For veterans relying on the VA formulary, the gap means potential delays in formulary additions for new imported drugs and higher costs for non-formulary medications.

What to Watch

Several developments will shape the trajectory:

  • September 29, 2026: Section 232 tariffs extend to all remaining pharmaceutical importers. This is the second and larger shock — far more companies than the original 17 Annex III firms will face the 100% default rate without agreements.
  • Generic drug tariff review: Commerce's one-year review deadline falls in early April 2027. If tariffs extend to generics, the cost impact shifts from branded drugs to the entire prescription system, where generics account for roughly 90% of prescriptions by volume.
  • Legal challenges: Section 232 operates under separate authority from the IEEPA tariffs struck down by the Supreme Court in February 2026, and multiple law firms confirm it remains intact. But legal challenges to scope and application are likely.
  • Section 122 renewal: The 150-day duration of the global surcharge brings its first renewal cycle around August 2026. Any modification changes the compound-duty math.
  • Canadian retaliation: If Canada responds with its own measures, U.S. pharmaceutical companies with cross-border supply chains face reciprocal costs.

The compound tariff regime now governing pharmaceutical imports is the most structurally complex in modern U.S. trade history. Understanding it is not optional for anyone who depends on prescription drug access — including the more than 9 million veterans enrolled in VA health care.

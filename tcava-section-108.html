ARTICLE BODY

A close look at Section 108 of the Take Care of America's Veterans Act — and why the hardest trade-offs in disability policy are never just about numbers.

There is a version of the Take Care of America's Veterans Act that almost everyone in the veterans policy community wants to see become law. It packages 62 bipartisan bills — including the Major Richard Star Act, which passed the House separately on September 15, 2026, via discharge petition, and would end the unjust practice of reducing disabled veterans' compensation simply because they also receive military retirement pay. It expands community care access. It invests in caregiver support and toxic exposure reform.

And then there is Section 108.

Section 108 would eliminate the compensable disability rating for tinnitus — the single most common VA disability claim, affecting approximately 2.5 million veterans — and sharply reduce ratings for sleep apnea, a condition that hundreds of thousands of veterans manage nightly with a CPAP machine. The estimated savings: $57–60 billion over ten years, redirected to fund the very benefits the bill promises.

The question this legislation forces is not whether these conditions deserve scrutiny. It is whether cutting one group of veterans' earned compensation to fund another group's benefits is a trade-off the system should make — and whether the process that produced this trade-off has earned the trust of the veterans it affects.

What Section 108 Actually Does

Under current law, tinnitus — ringing, buzzing, or hissing in the ears that veterans experience as a result of service-connected auditory damage — receives a 10 percent disability rating. It is modest compensation: roughly $180 per month at 2026 rates. But it is an acknowledgment that the condition exists, that it was caused by military service, and that it matters.

Section 108 would remove that compensable rating for the vast majority of veterans who experience tinnitus. The only exception: veterans whose tinnitus is associated with an otherwise noncompensable (0 percent) hearing loss rating. For everyone else — an estimated 1.5 million veterans currently receiving that compensation — the condition would be treated as a symptom of an underlying auditory disease rather than a standalone disability.

Sleep apnea ratings would shift even more dramatically. Today, veterans requiring a CPAP device receive a 50 percent rating, reflecting both the condition's severity and the dependence on treatment equipment. Under Section 108, ratings would be based on "responsiveness to treatment" — meaning that if a CPAP effectively manages the apnea, the rating could drop to 0 or 30 percent, regardless of the veteran's ongoing reliance on the device each night.

This is not a minor adjustment. It represents a fundamental change in VA disability philosophy: from compensating the condition to compensating only uncontrolled symptoms. Under this framework, effective treatment becomes a penalty — the better your CPAP works, the less the VA acknowledges you need it.

The Dual-Track Problem

Section 108 did not emerge in a vacuum. The VA has been pursuing parallel regulatory changes through the standard rulemaking process — proposed changes to the rating schedule for respiratory, auditory, and mental health conditions that would achieve similar outcomes administratively.

What Section 108 does is codify these changes in statute. That distinction matters. A regulatory change can be reversed by a future administration. A statutory change requires an act of Congress.

This dual-track approach creates a dilemma. Proponents, including the American Legion, argue that since the VA is already moving to reclassify these ratings, Congress should direct the savings toward veterans programs rather than letting them revert to the Treasury. It is a pragmatic argument — but it assumes the regulatory changes are inevitable, which is precisely what opponents contest.

The VSO Coalition Response

The opposition has been swift and unusually unified.

The VFW called Section 108 an ask that "future disabled veterans bear the cost of expanding benefits." The DAV labeled it a "poison pill." IAVA opposed trading one group's benefits for another's. The AFL-CIO condemned both the rating cuts and the bill's community care expansion, calling the latter "privatization by another name."

The American Legion's qualified support stands as the exception — and even that came with the caveat of two formal resolutions in opposition and 30 in favor.

On Capitol Hill, the picture is equally divided. HR 9237 has 30 cosponsors — all Republican. Zero Democrats have signed on. Forty-seven Senate Democrats and independents signed a letter opposing Section 108's codification of rating cuts. A House floor vote was canceled in late June after leadership determined there were insufficient votes to pass the bill in its current form. Leadership brought the bill back to the floor on July 16 under a new closed rule. The motion to recommit failed 210-211 (Roll Call 249), and the House then postponed further proceedings on the bill. H.R. 9237 passed the House on November 11, 2026 (Veterans Day), without a recorded vote amid controversy over Section 108's benefit cuts. The bill now moves to the Senate, where its prospects are uncertain. Veterans groups are split: the American Legion backs the bill; the VFW wants it stopped in the Senate; DAV condemned the fast-tracking as "unprecedented benefit cuts without recorded vote." The Major Richard Star Act also passed the House separately.

Meanwhile, the discharge petition for the standalone Major Richard Star Act (HR 2102) reached the 218-signature threshold on September 15, 2026, clearing the procedural path to force a floor vote — backed by 334 bipartisan cosponsors in the House. The contrast is instructive. The same legislators who broadly support expanded veterans benefits are refusing to do so at the cost of existing ones.

The Real Question for the Disability Evaluation System

The policy debate around Section 108 is often framed as a budget offset: you have to pay for new benefits somehow, and reclassifying ratings is one way to do it. That framing obscures a more important structural question.

When the disability evaluation system compensates a condition, it is making a statement about the relationship between military service and impairment. Tinnitus and sleep apnea are not marginal conditions. Tinnitus is the most prevalent service-connected disability in the VA system. Sleep apnea, particularly among post-9/11 veterans, has seen claim volumes increase dramatically — driven by higher prevalence linked to obesity, PTSD comorbidity, traumatic brain injury, and deployment-related factors, though the relative contribution of each is still being studied.

Downgrading these ratings says, in effect: we acknowledge your condition was caused by your service, but we have decided it is not disabling enough to compensate — or that your success in managing it means it no longer counts. Veterans experiencing hearing loss, veterans relying on CPAP devices every night, veterans who built their financial planning around earned benefits — they are not line items. They are people who were told a promise and acted on it.

Where This Goes Next

The legislation has passed the House and now faces its critical test in the Senate. On November 11, 2026 (Veterans Day), the House passed H.R. 9237 without a recorded vote — a controversial move that drew immediate criticism. Ranking Member Takano called it "the largest cut to veteran benefits in history." The bill now moves to the Senate, where 47 Democrats and independents have already signaled opposition to Section 108. Veterans groups are split: the American Legion backs the bill, while the VFW and DAV want it stopped or substantially amended. The Major Richard Star Act passed the House separately via its discharge petition victory. The near-term scenarios are: Senate passage with amendments to Section 108, Senate rejection, or the bill dying in committee. The VA's regulatory changes to the rating schedule proceed independently of TCAVA's legislative fate.

But here is what matters regardless of the legislative outcome: the VA's parallel regulatory track on rating reclassification is proceeding. The changes to how tinnitus and sleep apnea are evaluated may arrive through rulemaking even though Section 108 will not become law in this form. The difference is reversibility. A regulatory change can be challenged in court, reversed by a new administration, or modified through the notice-and-comment process. A statutory change cannot.

For veterans, for examiners, and for the professionals who navigate this system every day, the operational question is the same either way: prepare for change, monitor the Federal Register, and ensure that when the rating criteria shift, the humans affected by those shifts are not left to figure it out alone.

The disability evaluation system exists to translate a promise into a payment. When we start rewriting the translation to make the math work, we should at least be honest about whose compensation we are rewriting.

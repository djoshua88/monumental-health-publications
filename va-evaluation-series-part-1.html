The Map Before the Maze: How the VA Evaluates Mental Health Conditions

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 1 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

You are not supposed to know this already

There is a particular silence that sits around the VA disability evaluation process for mental health conditions. It is not hostility, exactly. It is more like a building where every door is unlabeled, the signs are in a language that was never taught to you, and the people inside seem to assume you arrived already knowing the floor plan.

You did not. Almost no one does.

I am a psychiatrist. I have spent years sitting across from veterans — in exam rooms, in records, in the slow work of trying to connect what happened to them in service with what is happening to them now. I also founded an organization that does independent medical opinions and nexus evaluations for veterans, which means I have spent a lot of time inside the machine that decides what a condition is "worth" on a rating sheet. I want to tell you what I have learned, not because I think the system is fair — it is not always fair — but because what you cannot see, you cannot navigate. And what you can see, you can.

This is the first of six installments. Each one builds on the last. By the end, you will have a mental model of the whole process — not so you can game it, but so you can meet it on equal footing. The system was not built with every veteran in mind. The least we can do is make the map readable.

Let us start with the one idea that, once you hold it, makes everything else make sense.

The VA does not rate your diagnosis. It rates your function.

If you remember nothing else from this series, remember this.

A diagnosis opens the door. A diagnosis is what tells the VA, "Yes, there is a recognized condition here, and it may be connected to service." But the rating — the percentage that determines the benefit — is not a measure of how much you suffer, or how hard you fought, or how real your pain is. The rating is a measure of how much the condition interferes with your functioning.

This is not a quibble. It is the central design principle of the entire mental health rating system.

Mental health disability ratings in the VA are governed by a single regulation — 38 C.F.R. § 4.130 — which contains what is called the General Rating Formula for Mental Disorders. That formula uses a ladder of percentages: 0, 10, 30, 50, 70, and 100. And at each rung, the regulation describes not a list of symptoms, but a level of occupational and social impairment.

Read that again. The rungs of the ladder are described in terms of impairment in work, in relationships, in thinking, in self-care, in safety — not in terms of which diagnosis you carry. Two veterans can have the exact same diagnosis and receive wildly different ratings, because what the VA is measuring is the effect, not the label.

This single fact explains more about the system than any other. It explains why a veteran who is plainly struggling can receive a low rating if their record never captured the functional impact. It explains why a veteran who seems "fine" to an examiner for forty-five minutes can be profoundly impaired in every other hour of the week and still get under-rated. It explains why the most important thing you can do for your own claim is learn to describe your life in the language the system is actually listening for.

We will spend the next installment on that language in detail — the five functional domains the regulation is built around. For now, hold the principle: function, not diagnosis, is what the rating measures.

Four moving parts (that repeat everywhere)

Once you grasp that function is the currency, the rest of the process is less mysterious than it looks. Stripped to its bones, the VA disability evaluation has four moving parts, and they recur at every stage:

1. A claim. A veteran asserts that a current condition is connected to service (or to an already service-connected condition). Everything starts here. No claim, no evaluation.

2. Evidence. The VA gathers and reviews records — service treatment records, post-service treatment records, lay statements, private medical opinions. The claim is a request; the evidence is the case.

3. An examination. In most mental health claims, the VA schedules a Compensation & Pension examination (a "C&P exam") — a VA examiner completes a Disability Benefits Questionnaire (DBQ) that documents diagnosis, symptoms, and, crucially, functional impact.

4. A rating decision. A rating specialist takes the evidence and the exam findings, applies § 4.130, and assigns a percentage. That percentage drives the benefit.

That is the whole machine. Claim, evidence, exam, decision. You will see these four parts again in every installment — when we talk about what the examiner is looking for (that is part 3), what the record should contain (that is parts 2 and 4), and what happens when the decision is wrong (that is the appeal, part 6). The parts do not change; only which one is in focus changes.

If a process feels overwhelming, return to the four parts. Almost every confusing letter, every opaque request for "more information," every long wait is the system moving through one of these four stages. Knowing which stage you are in is the first act of navigation.

Why this is harder for some veterans than others

I want to be honest about something that the official descriptions of this process tend to leave out.

The system assumes a veteran has continuity of records — a documented treatment history that traces a condition from service to the present. It assumes the veteran has access to care in the years after discharge, so that there is clinical evidence of the condition over time. It assumes the veteran can describe their own functional impairment in terms that map onto the regulation's language. And it assumes an examiner who has the time, training, and neutrality to capture that impairment accurately in a single visit.

None of those assumptions is equally true for every veteran.

A veteran who was discharged without stable access to care may have a years-long gap in their record — not because the condition was absent, but because the documentation was. A veteran whose community does not talk openly about mental health may never have sought treatment and may struggle to narrate impairment in the clinical vocabulary the exam expects. A veteran seen by an overworked examiner for thirty minutes may walk out with a DBQ that captures a fraction of their actual functioning. And a veteran who learned, in service, to push through and not complain may present as more functional than they are — because the very adaptation that kept them alive now works against them in the exam room.

These are not individual failings. They are structural features of a process that was designed for a particular kind of veteran with a particular kind of paper trail. Naming them is not an excuse; it is a diagnosis of the system, and it tells us where to put our effort. Across this series, I will keep returning to the places where the system structurally disadvantages veterans — record gaps, examiner variability, the cost of a well-reasoned medical opinion — because those are the places where informed advocacy changes outcomes.

A word on what this series is, and is not

This is not a substitute for an accredited VSO, an attorney, or a claims agent. It is not clinical advice, and it will not tell you what your rating "should" be. It will not opine on any individual's condition.

What it will do is give you the map — the structure of the process, the vocabulary of the regulation, and the leverage points where a well-prepared veteran, family member, or advocate can make the system work as it was supposed to. Knowledge of the system is not "gaming" the system. It is literacy. And in a process that too often rewards those who arrived already knowing the rules, literacy is a form of equity.

What comes next

In Installment 2, we will walk through the five functional domains the § 4.130 formula is actually built on — Occupational, Social, Cognitive, Self-Care, and Safety/Health — and the rule, from Bankhead v. Shulkin, that each domain is assessed independently. By the end of that piece, you will be able to locate your own experience inside the regulation's own language, which is the single most useful skill this series can give you.

For now, take these three things with you:

1. The VA rates function, not diagnosis. The door is a diagnosis; the room is your impairment.

2. The process has four parts — claim, evidence, exam, decision — and they repeat at every stage. Name the stage you are in, and the process becomes navigable.

3. The system was not built for every veteran equally. Knowing where it assumes continuity, access, and a particular kind of clinical language tells you exactly where to focus your energy — and where a good advocate is worth their weight.

You are not supposed to have known this already. Now you do.

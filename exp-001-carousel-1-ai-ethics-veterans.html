Slide 1 — Title Slide

AI ETHICS ISN'T ABSTRACT. FOR VETERANS, IT'S LIFE AND DEATH.

The algorithms that triage, diagnose, and prioritize care for 9 million VA patients weren't built in a vacuum. They were built on data that reflects every disparity in the system.

Here's why that matters — and what's at stake.

AI Ethics in Healthcare — Part 1 of 4

Slide 2 — The Scale

9 million veterans. 1 healthcare system. Algorithms making decisions at every level.

The VA is the largest integrated healthcare system in the United States. It uses predictive models for suicide risk (REACH VET), AI-assisted documentation tools, and algorithmic clinical decision support — all operating on data shaped by historical inequities.

When the data is biased, the algorithm doesn't fix the problem. It scales it.

Slide 3 — What "Algorithmic Bias" Actually Means

Algorithmic bias in healthcare isn't about prejudice in code.

It's about what the data doesn't see, misreads, or underweights — and then amplifies through automation.

Bias Type What Happens Who's Affected
Training data gaps Model trained on demographics that don't represent the patient population Women veterans, LGBTQ+ veterans, veterans of color
Proxy variables Cost used as a stand-in for health need — underestimates care needs for underserved groups Black patients, low-income veterans
Measurement error Devices calibrated for lighter skin miss conditions in darker skin Pulse oximetry, dermatology AI
Feedback loops Model predictions influence who gets follow-up, biasing future data Veterans already underserved by the system

Slide 4 — The REACH VET Case Study

REACH VET is the VA's flagship predictive analytics program. It identifies veterans at high risk for suicide using VHA health records.

Since 2017, it has flagged over 130,000 veterans for suicide prevention outreach.

But the original model considered being a white male a stronger indicator of suicide risk than factors primarily affecting women — and didn't account for LGBTQ+ identity at all.

Transgender veterans die by suicide at approximately 2x the rate of cisgender veterans. The model didn't see them.

In June 2025, REACH VET 2.0 added military sexual trauma (MST) and intimate partner violence (IPV) as risk variables — and removed race and ethnicity as predictors.

Better data. Better design. But the gap between 2017 and 2025 is measured in lives.

Slide 5 — The Disability Rating Dimension

The VA disability rating system determines compensation, access to care, and quality of life for millions of veterans.

These ratings are increasingly informed by algorithmic tools and standardized evaluation criteria that carry embedded assumptions:

  • TBI and mental health conditions are often rated as a single condition, reducing compensation and potentially misdirecting treatment
  • Sleep apnea ratings face proposed reduction under legislative reform (Section 108, TCAVA) — using "treatment effectiveness" as a rationale that penalizes veterans whose conditions improve because they received treatment
  • Rating criteria were developed using reference populations that don't reflect the demographics or service-connected conditions of today's veteran population

When the algorithm says you're less disabled than you are, the result isn't a data point. It's a denied benefit, a delayed treatment, a life made harder.

Slide 6 — Why Veterans Are Uniquely Vulnerable

Veterans face compounding risk factors that make biased algorithms more dangerous:

Factor Impact
Higher suicide rates ~17.5/day; post-9/11 veterans with TBI: up to 100 per 100,000
Compounding conditions TBI linked to PTSD, substance use, cardiovascular disease, elevated cancer mortality
System dependency VA is often the sole or primary healthcare provider
Limited alternatives If the algorithm gets it wrong, there's no second opinion for many veterans
Trust dynamics Veterans who've already experienced institutional betrayal are less likely to push back

A biased algorithm in a commercial health system might cost someone an appointment. In the VA, it can cost someone their life.

Slide 7 — The Trust Problem

Only 28% of the public is comfortable with AI scribes during mental health consultations.

For veterans — a population where trust is already fragile — the stakes are different:

  • Military sexual trauma survivors asked to have conversations recorded by AI
  • TBI patients whose cognitive symptoms might be misread by automated documentation
  • Veterans in crisis whose risk assessments are generated by models they've never been told about

Trust isn't a feature you can add later. It's the foundation you have to build first.

Slide 8 — Regulation Is Coming — But It's Reactive

States are racing to regulate AI in healthcare after high-profile failures:

Law State What It Does
AB 406 Nevada Prohibits AI chatbots from claiming to provide professional mental health services
SB 243 California Requires chatbots to detect suicidal ideation, disclose non-human nature, provide crisis referrals
HB 149 Texas Mandates explicit disclosure when AI is involved in healthcare services

These are important — and they're all reactive. They respond to failures that have already happened. They don't prevent the next one.

Slide 9 — What "Ethics by Design" Actually Looks Like

Ethics isn't a review board that signs off after the model is built. It's a set of design decisions made at every stage:

Data collection: Whose experiences are represented? Whose are missing? Model training: What variables predict risk — and what do they exclude? Validation: Was the model tested on the populations it will serve? Deployment: Can patients opt out? Do they know when AI is involved? Monitoring: Who watches for drift, and what happens when bias is detected? Governance: Who has the authority to pause or override an algorithmic decision?

If you can't answer these questions, the system isn't ready.

Slide 10 — The Framework: 5 Principles for AI That Serves Veterans

Principle What It Means What It Prevents
Representation Training data reflects the served population Undercounting risk for underserved groups
Transparency Patients and clinicians know when and how AI is used Black-box decisions with no accountability
Consent Opt-in, not opt-out; informed, not assumed Recording without knowledge (Sharp HealthCare lawsuit)
Accountability Clear liability when AI causes harm Nobody responsible when the algorithm fails
Continuous audit Ongoing bias testing and model recalibration Deploy-and-forget models that degrade over time

These aren't aspirational. They're operational requirements for any AI system that touches veteran lives.

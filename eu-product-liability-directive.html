EU Product Liability Directive: What AI-Health Companies Must Know Before December 9

Software and AI are now "products" under strict, no-fault liability. The transposition deadline is December 9, 2026.

On December 9, 2026, the European Union's revised Product Liability Directive must be transposed into national law across all 27 member states. For the first time, software and artificial intelligence are legally classified as "products" under a strict, no-fault liability regime. If your company builds AI-assisted clinical tools, diagnostic aids, patient-facing chatbots, or health data platforms, and any of those products touch an EU market, the liability calculus just changed.

The countdown is measured in days. As of this writing, we are inside the final five weeks.

What Changed

Directive (EU) 2024/2853, which entered into force on December 8, 2024, replaces the EU's 1985 Product Liability Directive, a framework written before smartphones, cloud computing, or generative AI existed. The old regime focused almost entirely on physical goods. Consumers harmed by software defects faced steep evidentiary barriers and, in most cases, could not recover damages at all.

The new directive closes that gap. Its core provisions:

Software and AI are now "products." The definition of a product explicitly includes stand-alone software, AI systems, cloud services, and integrated digital elements. Whether your AI tool runs on a local device or is accessed through a browser, it falls within scope. The one carve-out: free, open-source software developed outside a commercial activity is exempt, but the moment it is integrated into a commercial product, the integrating manufacturer bears full liability.

Strict, no-fault liability. Manufacturers of AI or software products are strictly liable for personal injury, medically recognized and medically certified psychological harm, and destruction or corruption of non-professional data. No showing of negligence is required. The claimant need only establish that the product was defective, that damage occurred, and that a causal link exists between the two.

No contract clause is opposable to the victim. Terms of service, liability waivers, and limitation-of-liability clauses in end-user agreements cannot be invoked against an injured party. This is a non-waivable protection. Your contracts still govern the relationship between you and your customers, but they provide no shield against a consumer claim.

Eased burden of proof for complex systems. When a claimant faces excessive difficulties proving defectiveness or causation due to the technical or scientific complexity of a product, particularly relevant for AI systems that operate as "black boxes," the directive establishes rebuttable presumptions that ease the claimant's evidentiary burden. The court may presume the product was defective and that the defect caused the harm. The manufacturer may then rebut the presumption by demonstrating the system was not defective or that the defect did not cause the damage.

Liability caps eliminated. The previous EU 500 threshold for property damage and national options for capping personal injury liability (such as Germany's EUR 85 million cap) are both removed. Claims are, in theory, uncapped.

Expanded compensable harm. The directive now covers medically certified psychological harm and the destruction or corruption of personal, non-professional data. For health-tech companies, this is significant. A defective AI tool that causes psychological harm, or that destroys or corrupts patient data, creates liability exposure that did not previously exist under the product liability regime.

Updates and modifications trigger new liability. A substantial modification or update to a product after December 9 may bring it within the scope of the new directive, even if the original product was placed on the market before that date.

Expanded chain of liable parties. If the manufacturer is outside the EU, strict liability extends to importers, authorized representatives, fulfillment service providers, and, under certain conditions, distributors. Online platforms that present products in a way that leads consumers to believe the platform is the supplier may also be held strictly liable.

What This Means for AI-Health Companies

The directive intersects with two other EU regulatory frameworks that health-tech companies are already navigating: the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), which defines risk-based requirements for AI systems, and the NIS2 Directive, which sets cybersecurity minimum standards. The product liability directive creates a civil-liability enforcement layer on top of both.

The practical effect is a compliance thicket with teeth. If your AI system violates an AI Act safety or transparency requirement, your product may be presumed "defective" in a civil liability proceeding. If you fail to meet NIS2 cybersecurity standards and a breach causes data destruction, your software could be presumed defectively designed. The regulatory violation becomes the foundation for the tort claim.

For US-based health-tech companies serving EU markets, including platforms that serve veterans, military families, or other populations through European partnerships or clinical trial sites, this creates several immediate considerations:

1. Map your EU exposure. Determine whether any of your AI products will be placed on the EU market or put into service on or after December 9, 2026. The transitional rule is clear: products placed on the market before December 9 remain under the old regime. But a substantial update or modification after that date may pull a legacy product into the new framework. If you ship a model update, a new clinical decision-support feature, or a revised diagnostic algorithm to EU users in 2027, the new strict liability regime likely applies.

2. Audit your documentation. The directive introduces disclosure obligations that allow plaintiffs to request evidence from defendants, a procedural mechanism previously unavailable in most EU member states. If a claimant makes a plausible claim, you may be required to disclose technical documentation, risk assessments, training data records, algorithm logic, and cybersecurity measures. If you fail to disclose, the court may presume the product was defective. Complete, centralized, audit-ready documentation is your primary defense against the eased burden of proof.

3. Review insurance coverage. Existing cyber liability and professional indemnity policies were likely priced before strict product liability for AI existed in the EU. With liability caps removed and compensable harm expanded to include psychological injury and data destruction, your coverage may be significantly inadequate. JPMorgan has warned that business and financial losses are not covered under the directive, meaning those costs fall on the company regardless. A coverage gap analysis is overdue.

4. Renegotiate vendor contracts. Recourse and indemnification clauses in your vendor and supplier agreements still apply between the parties to the contract. They do not apply against victims. But they do determine who bears the cost after a claim is paid. If your AI tool incorporates third-party models, open-source components, or cloud infrastructure, your indemnification chain needs to be reviewed before December 9, not after.

5. Implement defect logging and evidence retention. The directive establishes a three-year claim window from the date the claimant becomes aware of the damage, and a 10-year limitation period from the date the product was placed on the market (extended to 25 years for latent personal injuries). Your incident response, post-market monitoring, and evidence retention systems need to operate on those timelines.

The Broader Governance Picture

This directive does not exist in isolation. It is part of an accelerating global trend toward holding AI manufacturers accountable for the outcomes their systems produce. In the United States, more than 115 AI-related laws have been enacted across 30 states in 2026, including Colorado's restrictions on AI in psychotherapy and New York's ban on unsafe chatbot behavior with minors. Federal legislation has stalled, but state-level regulation is filling the vacuum.

For organizations committed to responsible AI governance, the EU directive is less a surprise than a confirmation. The principles it encodes, transparency, accountability, documentation, and victim protection, are the same principles that clinical AI governance frameworks have been advocating for years. The difference is that compliance is now enforced through civil liability with no cap and no waiver.

What to Do This Week

With 38 days remaining, the window for preparation is narrow but real. The organizations that will navigate this transition most effectively are the ones that treat it as a governance priority rather than a legal footnote. Three actions for this week:

First, convene your legal, compliance, engineering, and product teams to identify every AI product that could be subject to the directive. Second, commission a coverage gap analysis from your insurance broker. Third, ensure your technical documentation can withstand a disclosure request.

December 9 is not a deadline for planning. It is a deadline for having already acted.

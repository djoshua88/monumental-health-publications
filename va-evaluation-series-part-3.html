The Evidence You Bring vs. The Evidence You Need

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 3 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

Two piles on the same desk

In the first installment, I gave you the map — four parts that repeat at every stage: claim, evidence, exam, decision. In the second, I took you inside the rating formula and showed you the five functional domains where the rating actually lives: Occupational, Social, Cognitive, Self-Care, and Safety/Health.

Now I want to talk about the part that trips up more veterans than any other: the evidence.

Here is what I see, sitting on the same desk, in case after case. A veteran comes in with a folder — sometimes physical, sometimes digital — and they believe, reasonably, that this folder is their claim. It contains their DD-214, some service treatment records, maybe a few post-service therapy notes, perhaps a prior VA exam report. They assume the system will read it as the story of what happened and what it cost.

That folder is real, and it matters. But it is almost never the whole case. The gap between what is in that folder and what the VA needs to decide the claim is where most claims stall — not because the veteran did something wrong, but because nobody told them what was missing.

That is what this installment is about: the evidence you bring, the evidence you need, and how to tell the difference.

What you bring: the record that already exists

Let us start with what most veterans already have, or can reasonably obtain. I will call this the evidence you bring — the documentation that exists before anyone writes a new opinion or schedules a new exam.

Service treatment records and personnel files

These are the records generated during your time in service: entrance exams, sick-call visits, mental health contacts, disciplinary actions, performance evaluations, deployment records, and the DD-214. They are the backbone of any direct service connection claim because they establish the in-service event — the thing that happened in service that you are now saying is connected to your current condition.

Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303, direct service connection requires three elements: (1) a current diagnosis, (2) an in-service event, injury, or disease, and (3) a medical nexus connecting the two. Service treatment records and personnel files are where element two lives. Without them — or without an adequate substitute — the first domino does not fall.

Post-service treatment records

If you received mental health treatment after discharge — through the VA, community providers, or private clinicians — those records are evidence of your current condition and its longitudinal course. They show the condition exists, has persisted, and has functional consequences. This is where element one (current diagnosis) and part of the functional impairment picture come from.

Treatment records are powerful because they are contemporaneous — written by a clinician treating you, not someone constructing a case. But they are incomplete: treating clinicians document for care, not for the VA rating formula. They rarely use the five-domain language from § 4.130. They note symptoms and medication adjustments; they do not typically document occupational impairment, social functioning, cognitive deficits, self-care decline, or safety/health concerns in the regulatory framework the rating specialist needs.

Prior C&P exam reports and rating decisions

If you have been through the VA evaluation process before — for this condition or another — the prior DBQ and rating decision are part of your record. They establish baselines and document prior findings. A prior exam that found no suicidal ideation, followed by a current exam that finds it, is not just a data point. It is a trajectory, and trajectories matter.

Your own statement

Your own written statement — sometimes called a lay statement or statement in support of claim — is evidence. This is a point many veterans miss. Under long-standing VA law, lay evidence is competent for things a person can personally observe: symptoms, behavior changes, functional decline, timing of onset. What lay evidence cannot do is opine on medical causation — that requires medical expertise, as Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004), established. But for everything short of the medical nexus conclusion, your own words carry weight.

What you need: the evidence that has to be created

Now here is the harder part. The evidence you bring is necessary but rarely sufficient. To get from "I have a condition and it started in service" to "the VA has granted service connection at a rating that reflects my actual impairment," most veterans need evidence that does not yet exist — documentation that must be commissioned, requested, or written specifically for the claim.

A current Disability Benefits Questionnaire (DBQ)

A DBQ is the structured form the VA uses to document a condition against the § 4.130 criteria. If you have a C&P exam scheduled, the examiner will complete one. But you can also have a private clinician complete a DBQ — and a well-completed private DBQ, done by a clinician who has the time to assess all five domains thoroughly, can be among the most influential documents in your file.

The DBQ is where the five-domain language from Installment 2 gets applied to your case. It is where "sometimes I think about whether it would be easier if I weren't here" becomes "suicidal ideation, passive, several times per week, most recent episode within the past month" — the regulatory language that maps to a 50% or 70% tier instead of 10% or 30%.

A nexus opinion

This is the piece that connects your current condition to your service. For direct service connection under § 3.303, the nexus is the link between the in-service event and the current diagnosis. For secondary service connection under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310, the nexus is the link between a service-connected condition and a new or aggravated condition.

A nexus opinion is a medical opinion — written by a qualified clinician — that reviews the record, applies clinical reasoning, and states whether it is "at least as likely as not" (50% probability or greater) that the connection exists. Under Shedden v. Principi, this must be medical evidence, not lay assertion. Under the Adams v. Nicholson multi-causation doctrine, the opinion need only establish that the service-connected condition is a cause, not the cause, when multiple factors are involved.

This is the piece most veterans do not have and do not know they need — and the piece that, in my experience, changes outcomes more than any other. A well-reasoned nexus opinion supplies the element the VA cannot construct from the existing record alone.

Buddy statements and family statements

Statements from people who knew you during service — fellow service members, roommates, supervisors — can establish the in-service event, especially when service treatment records are thin or absent. Statements from family members, partners, and close friends can document the functional decline the five domains measure: the veteran who used to be social and now isolates; the veteran who was reliable at work and now cannot hold a schedule; the veteran who was composed and now has explosive episodes.

These statements are lay evidence, competent for what a layperson can observe. They cannot diagnose or opine on causation. But they can witness — and in mental health claims, where the rating is about function, the people who see you every day are often your most credible witnesses.

The gap, and who falls into it

Here is where the equity lens matters, and where I have to be honest about what the system assumes and who it leaves behind.

The evidence you bring assumes continuity — that service records exist, were preserved, and can be retrieved. That assumption is not equally true for every veteran.

The 1973 National Personnel Records Center fire destroyed approximately 16–18 million military personnel files, including an estimated 80% of Army records for veterans discharged between 1912 and 1960. Veterans whose records were destroyed — and their survivors — face a structural documentation gap that is not their fault and that the VA is required to work around. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.159, the VA has a duty to assist claimants in obtaining evidence, including making reasonable efforts to obtain relevant records. When records are lost, the VA is supposed to accept alternative evidence — but the veteran has to know that alternative evidence is allowed, and they have to produce it. Military Sexual Trauma (MST) creates a different gap. MST-related claims are often missing the one thing other claims rely on: a contemporaneous report. Sexual assault in service is chronically underreported — shame, fear of retaliation, institutional barriers, and the power dynamics of military service all suppress documentation. The VA recognizes this and allows "markers" — behavioral changes, increased sick-call visits, substance use changes, performance declines, requests for transfer — as evidence that the trauma occurred. But the veteran has to know that markers exist and how to document them. A buddy statement describing a sudden change in behavior after a specific date can be the evidence that makes an MST claim viable — but only if someone helps the veteran write it. Veterans from communities with mental health stigma face a different version of the same gap. If you never sought treatment during service because seeking treatment was culturally punished, your service treatment records will show nothing — not because the condition was absent, but because the disclosure path was blocked. If you did not receive post-service care because you lacked insurance, lived in a provider desert, or could not afford co-pays, your post-service records will be thin — again, not because the condition resolved, but because the documentation infrastructure was absent. Under § 3.304(b), the presumption of soundness means you are presumed to have been in sound condition at service entry unless the VA can show clear and unmistakable evidence to the contrary. That presumption is a right, but it only helps if your claim is filed and your alternative evidence is presented.

These are not edge cases. They are the cases I see most often. And in every one, the gap between the evidence the veteran brings and the evidence the claim needs is not a personal failure — it is a systemic feature, and naming it is the first step in closing it.

The duty to assist — and its limits

Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.159, the VA has a statutory duty to assist claimants in developing claims — obtaining service treatment records, scheduling a C&P exam, providing medical examinations. The duty is real, and it matters.

But the duty to assist is not a duty to build your case for you. The VA will obtain records it can find and schedule an exam. What it will not do is commission a well-reasoned independent medical opinion that supplies nexus with the correct probability language, or help you write a buddy statement documenting functional decline in five-domain terms, or ensure your lay statement uses the regulatory vocabulary that maps your experience onto the § 4.130 ladder.

That is the gap where advocacy lives — where a trained VSO, a thorough clinician, or a well-prepared veteran can close it, and closing it changes outcomes.

What to take with you

1. Your existing record is the foundation, not the whole case. Service treatment records, personnel files, post-service notes, prior exam reports, and your own statement are the evidence you bring — necessary, but rarely sufficient.

2. Service connection requires three elements under § 3.303: a current diagnosis, an in-service event, and a medical nexus. The evidence you bring often covers the first two. The evidence you need — a nexus opinion — supplies the third.

3. A current DBQ is where functional impairment gets translated into the rating formula. Whether from a C&P exam or a private clinician, the DBQ is where the five-domain language from Installment 2 gets applied to your life.

4. Buddy and family statements are competent evidence for what a layperson can observe. In mental health claims, where the rating is about function, the people who see you every day are often your strongest witnesses.

5. The system assumes continuity that not every veteran has. Lost records, MST-related documentation gaps, and structural barriers to care create gaps that are not personal failings. The VA's duty to assist under § 3.159 is real but limited — and the evidence you create is where advocacy changes outcomes.

6. Under § 3.304(b), you are presumed to have been in sound condition when you entered service. That presumption is a right. The absence of a pre-service mental health diagnosis is not a gap you have to explain — it is the default the law assumes.

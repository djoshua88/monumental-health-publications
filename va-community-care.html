VA Community Care Is Now 40% of Medical Spending

The fastest-growing segment of federal healthcare is not a new program. It is a structural shift in how the VA delivers care — and the next 12 months will determine who controls it.

Something important has been happening in veterans healthcare, and it is not the thing most people are watching.

While Congress debates the Take Care of America's Veterans Act and the VA navigates its latest round of rating schedule changes, community care — the program that allows veterans to see non-VA providers when the VA cannot deliver timely or sufficient care — has quietly become the dominant delivery channel for VA healthcare.

In FY2026, community care appropriations are projected to reach $33–34 billion. That is roughly a 50 percent increase over FY2025 discretionary levels, and it means community care now accounts for more than 40 percent of total VA medical care spending — up from approximately 30 percent just eight years ago.

This is not a marginal program anymore. It is the program. And understanding what comes next requires understanding three forces: policy, demand, and market structure.

The Policy Accelerant

Two legislative changes are reducing friction in the community care referral pipeline — and they are doing it now.

The Dole Act (P.L. 118-210), signed in January 2025, eliminated the second-approval requirement for community care referrals. Previously, a veteran's referring clinician had to get a second VA physician to sign off before a community care referral could proceed. That extra step is gone. The result: lower barriers to referral and, inevitably, higher volume.

Starting in August 2025, 30 standardized service types now receive 12-month authorizations instead of the previous 90- to 180-day renewal cycles. This means longer, more predictable revenue windows for community care providers and less administrative overhead for VA staff. It also means more veterans flowing through community care pipelines — and staying there longer.

The GAO has flagged gaps in wait-time monitoring and network adequacy tracking, with only 9 of 27 recommendations implemented as of February 2026. Congressional pressure to improve community care quality metrics creates a structural incentive for quality-differentiated providers.

The Demand Surge

Behind these policy changes is a demand story that makes them urgent rather than aspirational.

Over 2.36 million PACT Act claims have been processed through March 2026, with more than 100,000 veterans enrolling in VA healthcare in early 2026 alone. These veterans disproportionately need specialty care — pulmonology, oncology, mental health — that the VA cannot provide internally at scale.

The behavioral health gap is the clearest illustration. According to VA and DoD workforce data, only 11 to 22 percent of psychiatrist workforce needs are met in key states. Virginia meets just 22.4 percent of recommended psychiatrist staffing. West Virginia faces similar shortages. The APA Monitor warned in March 2026 that VA workforce shortages threaten veterans' mental healthcare access.

These are not abstract statistics. They are the reason community care exists. When the VA cannot staff a psychiatrist within the 30-minute, 20-day access standard for mental health, the referral goes to the community. The gap is not narrowing — it is widening as PACT Act enrollment grows.

VA claims processing has accelerated: the backlog dropped 69 percent from January 2025 to April 2026, and average processing time fell from 141.5 days to 75.7 days. Faster processing means faster referrals. But the claims-level accuracy rate of 83.31 percent means roughly 500,000 error-containing claims per year — creating re-referrals and remands that further strain the system.

The Market Reconfiguration

The community care market is about to undergo its most significant structural change since the MISSION Act.

The current Community Care Network (CCN) contracts, held by Optum (Regions 1–3, covering 36 states plus D.C. and two territories) and TriWest (Regions 4–5, covering 14 states plus two territories), expire in 2026. The CCN Next Generation recompete — Solicitation 36C10G26R0003, released March 6, 2026 — consolidates five regions into two: East and West.

A single contractor in each region will control provider credentialing, referral authorization, claims adjudication, scheduling, ML-based fraud detection, and value-based care mandates. The PWS includes 17 task areas and up to $8.2 million in annual disincentives per region for performance failures.

This is not a routine contract renewal. The 5-to-2 consolidation concentrates gatekeeping power. The winning TPAs will determine which providers enter the network, how referrals are routed, and how quality metrics are enforced. Incumbents are recompeting; potential new entrants could reshape regional dynamics. Award is expected mid-2026.

For small and set-aside contractors — including veteran-owned, 8(a), and HUBZone firms — the entry points are specific and time-sensitive. Veterans Care Agreements (VCAs) offer the lowest barrier: a direct agreement between a community provider and the local VAMC, used when CCN services are unavailable or insufficient. CCN Next Gen subcontracting depends on who wins each region and requires early engagement with prime contractors. SDVOSB certification carries the highest set-aside value under VA's Veterans First contracting priority (38 U.S.C. § 8127). The behavioral health gap makes psychiatric and mental health services the most compelling entry wedge — aligning community care referral volume with existing C&P examination capabilities.

What This Means — And What It Doesn't

Community care's growth is a fact. The projection that it will consume over 40 percent of VA medical care spending in FY2026 is sourced from House and Senate appropriations bills, the VA's own budget submission, and VA OIG reporting on the $12 billion supplemental funding request that revealed systematic underbudgeting.

What is not yet determined is the quality and equity of that care. The GAO's unimplemented recommendations — on wait-time monitoring, network adequacy, and metrics alignment — represent a gap between spending and accountability. The CCN Next Gen recompete will determine whether the next generation of community care prioritizes volume or outcomes. And the behavioral health workforce shortage means that even with perfect policy, the system cannot deliver what veterans need without enough providers.

The next 12 months — the CCN Next Gen award, the Dole Act's full implementation, continued PACT Act enrollment growth — will set the trajectory for a decade. The question is not whether community care will grow. It already has. The question is whether it will grow in a way that serves the veterans who depend on it — or simply grows.

Community care is not a side program. It is where nearly half of VA healthcare spending is going. The structural decisions being made right now — about who controls access, how quality is measured, and whether the system can actually deliver on its referral promise — will determine whether that spending translates into care.

The Link: Nexus, Medical Opinions, and Why "At Least as Likely as Not" Matters

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 5 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

The part that feels like Latin

If you have been reading from the beginning, you now have the map and the vocabulary. You know the four moving parts — claim, evidence, exam, decision — and that they repeat at every stage (Installment 1). You know the VA rates function, not diagnosis, under 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, across five domains (Installment 2). You know the C&P exam is one room in the house, not the whole house (Installment 3). And you know that a claim is a body of evidence read as a whole (Installment 4).

Now we arrive at the part that, in my experience, causes more confusion and more silent defeats than any other. It is the link. The legal connection between a current condition and service — or between a current condition and an already service-connected condition. The word the system uses is nexus. And the standard the system applies is a phrase that sounds like a riddle: "at least as likely as not."

One reason nexus defeats so many claims is that it is the one element lay evidence cannot supply. As we saw in Installment 4, lay evidence — your own statement, buddy statements, family statements — is competent for what a layperson can observe: symptoms, behavior, functional decline. But it cannot opine on medical causation. That line was drawn in Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163 (Fed. Cir. 2004), which held that establishing the causal link between a current disability and in-service events generally requires medical evidence. You can witness that you changed after deployment. You cannot witness why — not in the medical-legal sense the nexus element demands. That is why nexus, more than any other element, turns on a medical opinion.

This is the part that feels like Latin. Here is the translation.

What nexus actually means

Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303, direct service connection requires three elements: a current diagnosis, an in-service event, and a medical nexus linking the two. You can have the diagnosis. You can have the in-service event — documented in your service treatment records, established by a buddy statement, presumed under the right circumstances. But if the record does not contain a medical link between the current condition and service, the claim stalls at the third element. Nexus is that link. It is the medical conclusion that says: this condition, which exists now, is connected to what happened then.

Here is the critical point that veterans almost never hear: the standard is not certainty. The VA's nexus standard is "at least as likely as not," which means a probability of 50 percent or greater. Equal equipoise. If the evidence for and against the link is balanced — if it is a coin flip — the law resolves the doubt in the veteran's favor. That is the reasonable doubt rule, codified at 38 C.F.R. § 4.3, and it applies to service connection determinations just as it applies to rating questions. It is not a courtesy. It is the law.

This matters enormously in mental health claims, where causation is rarely clean. A veteran's current depression may be connected to an in-service trauma, but also have genetic, developmental, or post-service contributors. The question the VA is asking is not "did service cause this and nothing else." It is: is it at least as likely as not — 50 percent or greater — that service (or a service-connected condition) is a cause?

That question is answerable even when the picture is complicated. And the way it gets answered is through a medical opinion that does the reasoning out loud.

Multi-causation: a cause, not the cause

This is where mental health nexus diverges from the way most people — including some clinicians — think about causation.

In physical injury claims, causation is often binary: the event either caused the injury or it did not. Mental health is different. A current mental health condition is almost always the product of multiple factors — temperament, early experience, genetics, exposure, cumulative stress, loss. When a veteran develops depression years after service, the honest clinical question is not "did service cause this." It is "is the service-connected condition a contributing cause among others?"

The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims answered this directly in Adams v. Nicholson, 22 Vet. App. 94 (2006). The court held that for secondary service connection under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a), the service-connected condition need only be a cause of the secondary condition — not the sole cause, not the primary cause, not the predominant cause. A cause. One contributing factor among others is sufficient, so long as the contribution is more than negligible.

This is not a loophole. It is a recognition of how mental health conditions actually develop. And it changes the kind of medical opinion that can support a claim. An opinion that says "service-connected PTSD is the sole cause of this veteran's depression" is making a claim the evidence rarely supports — and an opinion that overstates its case is vulnerable on appeal. An opinion that says "it is at least as likely as not that service-connected PTSD is a cause, though not the sole cause, of the veteran's current depression" is making a defensible, evidence-integrated claim. The second opinion is stronger than the first, even though its language is more cautious. It is stronger because it is honest, and honest opinions survive scrutiny.

What an independent medical opinion does

An independent medical opinion — an IMO — is a private, evidence-based medical opinion that a veteran commissions to supply or strengthen nexus. I have spent a significant part of my career building these, which is the founding context for Monumental Health. I want to be clear about what an IMO is and what it is not, because there is a lot of confusion and some cynicism about them.

An IMO is not a favorable conclusion purchased from a willing clinician. The market contains opinions of varying quality, and a veteran who shops for a conclusion rather than for reasoning is wasting their money — an opinion that reaches a favorable result without explaining why can be discounted by the rating specialist as conclusory. The value of an IMO lies in its reasoning and evidence integration, not in its conclusion alone.

A well-reasoned IMO does four things:

1. It reviews the relevant record. Service treatment records, post-service treatment records, prior C&P exams, prior rating decisions, lay statements. The opinion should demonstrate that the reviewer actually read the file, not that they glanced at a summary.

2. It names the mechanism. Not just "PTSD caused depression" but the clinical pathway — chronic hyperarousal and sleep disruption eroding affective regulation, social withdrawal removing protective factors, cumulative stress exceeding coping capacity. The mechanism is what makes the opinion medical rather than administrative.

3. It integrates the record into the reasoning. The opinion should connect specific record evidence to the mechanism — the sleep complaints in the post-service treatment notes, the social withdrawal in the spouse's lay statement, the occupational decline in the C&P exam. This is where the evidence framing from Installment 4 earns its second life: the same body of evidence that establishes functional impairment also supports the nexus reasoning when a clinician integrates it.

4. It uses the correct probability language. The standard is "at least as likely as not" (50% or greater). In multi-causation cases, the Adams qualifier — "a cause, though not the sole cause" — must be present. An opinion that says "more likely than not" without the Adams qualifier in a multi-causation case may be read as claiming sole causation it cannot support. An opinion that uses vague language — "there appears to be a connection" or "it is possible that service contributed" — fails the standard entirely. "Possible" is not "at least as likely as not."

A vague IMO is weak regardless of its conclusion. A well-reasoned IMO that reaches an unfavorable conclusion is still more useful than a vague favorable one — because it tells you where the case actually stands and what evidence is missing, while the vague favorable opinion gives false confidence that dissolves under review.

Where equity enters

I want to return to something I raised in Installment 4, because it converges here.

Veterans without access to continuity records or specialty evaluations are the ones most often told their nexus is "insufficient." Their service treatment records are thin or destroyed. Their post-service treatment has gaps — because they lacked insurance, lived in a provider desert, or came from a community where seeking mental health care was punished. And when the rating specialist looks for the medical link, the record does not contain one — not because the link does not exist, but because no one with medical authority ever examined the evidence and wrote the reasoning down.

This is the structural barrier. The nexus requirement is not unreasonable — the VA is entitled to a medical link before it connects a condition to service. But the burden of producing that link falls hardest on the veterans least equipped to carry it: those whose records are thinnest, whose access to specialty evaluation has been most limited, and who have been out of service the longest.

A reasoned IMO is the instrument that most often changes this. It is the mechanism by which a veteran with a fragmented record can still present a defensible medical link — because the IMO's job is to take whatever evidence exists, integrate it with clinical reasoning, and produce the nexus conclusion the record itself never contained. For a veteran whose record is a decade of gaps and a forty-five-minute exam, an IMO is often the difference between an under-developed claim and a decided one. This is not a deficit framing. It is a leverage framing — the veteran who understands that nexus is the missing piece, and that a reasoned opinion can supply it, has a tool the system assumes they do not know about.

What to take with you

1. Nexus is the legal link between a current condition and service (or a service-connected condition), and the standard is "at least as likely as not" — 50 percent or greater, not certainty. When the evidence is in equipoise, the reasonable doubt rule (38 C.F.R. § 4.3) resolves it in the veteran's favor. This is the law, not a favor.

2. Mental health nexus is multi-causation, not sole causation. Under Adams v. Nicholson, 22 Vet. App. 94 (2006), a service-connected condition need only be a cause — not the cause — of a secondary condition. An opinion that claims sole causation it cannot support is weaker than one that uses the Adams qualifier honestly.

3. An IMO's value lies in reasoning and evidence integration — not in a favorable conclusion alone. A well-reasoned IMO reviews the record, names the mechanism, integrates the evidence, and uses correct probability language. A vague IMO is weak regardless of its conclusion.

4. Correct probability language matters. "At least as likely as not" is the standard. In multi-causation cases, the Adams qualifier ("a cause, though not the sole cause") is required. "Possible" is not "at least as likely as not."

5. Equity: veterans with the thinnest records are the ones most often told their nexus is insufficient — and for whom a reasoned IMO most often changes an outcome. The nexus burden falls hardest where the documentation infrastructure is weakest. A reasoned IMO is the instrument that closes that gap.

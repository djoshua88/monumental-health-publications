Secondary Conditions and Appeals: When One Condition Leads to Another, and When the Answer Is "No"

Demystifying the VA Mental Health Disability Evaluation — Part 6 of 6
By Joshua Anthony, MD — Psychiatrist and Founder, Monumental Health
Educational content, not clinical advice. This series does not diagnose, treat, or opine on any individual's condition. All examples are hypothetical and illustrative. Veterans seeking to file or appeal a claim should consult an accredited Veterans Service Officer (VSO), attorney, or claims agent accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel.

The cascade, and the recourse

If you have been reading from the beginning, you now have the map and the vocabulary. You know the four moving parts — claim, evidence, exam, decision — and that they repeat at every stage (Installment 1). You know the VA rates function, not diagnosis, under 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, across five domains (Installment 2). You know the C&P exam is one room in the house, not the whole house (Installment 3). You know a claim is a body of evidence read as a whole (Installment 4). And you know that nexus is the legal link between a current condition and service, and that the standard is "at least as likely as not" — 50 percent or greater probability, not certainty (Installment 5).

This final installment takes the nexus concept one step further, into secondary conditions — the cascade when a service-connected condition causes or worsens a new condition. Then it covers what happens when the answer is "no" — the appeal system that exists because first decisions are often incomplete, not because the veteran was wrong. And it ties all six installments together.

When one condition leads to another

A service-connected condition does not just sit there. It has consequences. A service-connected knee injury changes how you walk, which changes your back. A service-connected mental health condition affects your sleep, your relationships, your cardiovascular system. The VA recognizes this. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a), a condition proximately due to, or the result of, a service-connected disease or injury shall itself be service-connected. This is secondary service connection — the secondary condition can be rated on its own, with its own diagnostic code and percentage.

This is not a loophole. It is a recognition that the body and mind do not live in separate boxes. A service-connected condition radiates outward, and the VA's own regulation accounts for that.

Consider a synthetic example — entirely hypothetical. A veteran has service-connected PTSD, rated at 50 percent. Over time, he develops major depressive disorder, emerging in the context of chronic hyperarousal, sleep disruption, social withdrawal — the downstream effects of the PTSD. Under § 3.310(a), the depression can be claimed as a secondary condition. Under Adams v. Nicholson, 22 Vet. App. 94 (2006), the service-connected condition need only be a cause — not the sole cause. If the PTSD is a contributing cause, and the nexus opinion uses the correct Adams language — "at least as likely as not that service-connected PTSD is a cause, though not the sole cause, of the veteran's current major depressive disorder" — the secondary condition can be service-connected and rated. Critically, the rating reflects the full severity of the secondary condition, not the proportion attributable to the primary condition. An Adams claim is not a fractional claim.

Aggravation is not causation

Now a harder distinction. Sometimes a service-connected condition does not cause a new condition. Instead, it aggravates a condition that already existed. And the law treats aggravation differently from causation.

Under Spicer v. Nicholson, 22 Vet. App. 245 (2007), when a service-connected condition aggravates a non-service-connected condition beyond its natural progression, that aggravation is compensable under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(b). The key phrase is "beyond its natural progression." The question is not whether the non-SC condition exists — it does. The question is whether the service-connected condition made it worse than it otherwise would have been.

This is fundamentally different from causation. In a causation claim, you ask whether the SC condition brought a new condition into existence. In an aggravation claim, you ask whether the SC condition worsened an existing condition beyond the trajectory it was already on. And the compensation reflects that difference: under Spicer, the rating covers the degree of aggravation — the difference between the baseline severity and the current, worsened severity — not the full condition.

Here is a synthetic example. A veteran has service-connected PTSD and non-service-connected essential hypertension — high blood pressure diagnosed before PTSD onset, controlled with a single medication, Stage 1. After PTSD onset, the hypertension progresses to Stage 2, requiring two medications, with persistently elevated readings despite treatment. The question is whether the PTSD aggravated the hypertension beyond its natural progression. If the nexus opinion establishes the baseline (Stage 1), the current severity (Stage 2, poorly controlled), the aggravation mechanism (chronic sympathetic activation, sleep disruption), and distinguishes the current severity from expected natural progression — then under Spicer, the veteran is entitled to a rating for the degree of aggravation. Not for the full hypertension. For the difference.

There is a parallel doctrine. Under Allen v. Principi, 237 F.3d 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2001), the Federal Circuit held that secondary service connection is available for conditions — including those involving voluntary misconduct like alcohol or drug abuse — where a service-connected condition was a contributing cause. This principle extends to aggravation: when an in-service event aggravates a pre-existing condition beyond its natural progression, that aggravation is compensable. Allen addresses secondary service connection where the service-connected condition is a contributing cause; Spicer addresses aggravation by a service-connected condition of a non-service-connected condition. The distinction matters because it determines the legal framework and what must be proven. But the core principle is the same: the rating covers the degree of aggravation, not the full condition. If you take away one thing from this section, take that.

Why does this matter so much? Because an opinion that treats an aggravation claim as a causation claim — or that rates the full condition instead of the degree of aggravation — is vulnerable. Our clinical team's desk references make this explicit: the decision tree for secondary service connection evaluations requires selecting the correct doctrine before writing the nexus opinion. An Adams claim gets a full rating for the secondary condition; a Spicer claim gets a rating only for the degree of aggravation. Getting this right is not a technicality. It determines what the veteran receives.

When the answer is "no"

Now the part of the system that most veterans meet first: the denial.

A rating decision arrives. It says the claim is denied, or granted at a lower rating than expected. The letter is dense and formal. It is not a verdict on the veteran's credibility. A denial is a decision made on the evidence in the file at that time, by a rating specialist who may have had incomplete information, an under-developed record, or a nexus opinion that used the wrong language. It is not a finding that the veteran was wrong. It is a finding that the case, as it stood, did not meet the threshold.

The system knows this. That is why the appeal system exists. Under 38 U.S.C. § 5104C (the statutory authority for the three review lanes under the Appeals Modernization Act), a veteran who disagrees with a decision has three primary lanes:

1. Supplemental Claim (38 C.F.R. § 3.2501). The path when you have new and relevant evidence — a new treatment record, a new lay statement, a new independent medical opinion. The VA has a duty to assist, and the claim gets a fresh review with the new evidence. This is often the right first step when the original claim was denied for insufficient evidence.

2. Higher-Level Review (38 C.F.R. § 3.2601). The path when you believe the decision was wrong on the evidence already in the file. A senior reviewer examines the same record and can overturn the decision. You can request an informal phone discussion to explain why the decision was incorrect. This is right when the evidence was there but the decision did not account for it properly.

3. Board Appeal (38 C.F.R. § 20.202). The path that takes the decision out of the Regional Office and places it before a Veterans Law Judge at the Board of Veterans' Appeals. The Board can grant, deny, or remand — send it back with instructions to correct a specific error. A remand is not a denial; it is the system saying, "This was not done correctly; go back and do it right."

These three lanes are not a hierarchy you must climb in order. A veteran can choose the lane that fits the situation. The point is that a first decision is not the end. It is one decision, made on one version of the record, by one person. The appeal system exists because the system itself recognizes that first decisions are often incomplete — and that a veteran who comes back with better evidence, or who asks for a second look, is not gaming the process. They are using it as designed.

I want to name the equity dimension, because it runs through this series. Veterans with the thinnest records — those who lacked access to continuity care, who came from communities where seeking mental health treatment was stigmatized, who were discharged into provider deserts — are the ones most often denied on the first pass. For these veterans, the appeal system is not a formality. It is the mechanism by which a claim under-developed through no fault of the veteran can be built into a complete case. The appeal is where the leverage is — and where informed advocacy matters most.

The reasonable doubt rule, one more time

Under 38 C.F.R. § 4.3, when the evidence is in equipoise — when it is balanced, a coin flip — the doubt resolves in the veteran's favor. This is not a courtesy. It is the law. It applies to secondary service connection determinations, to aggravation claims, to appeals. If the evidence for and against secondary service connection is equally balanced, the claim is granted. The reasonable doubt rule is the structural answer to the argument that the system is stacked against the veteran: the law itself says that when the case is a tie, the veteran wins. The job of a well-prepared claim — and a well-reasoned opinion — is to get the case to equipoise. Once you are there, the law does the rest.

The whole system, one last time

The VA disability evaluation for mental health conditions is a system with four moving parts — claim, evidence, exam, decision — repeating at every stage. It rates function, not diagnosis, under 38 C.F.R. § 4.130, across five domains assessed independently. A claim is a body of evidence read as a whole. Nexus is the legal link, and the standard is "at least as likely as not" — 50 percent or greater. Under Adams, a service-connected condition need only be a cause, not the sole cause, of a secondary condition. A service-connected condition can cause or aggravate a new condition under § 3.310, and aggravation — under Spicer and Allen — covers only the degree of aggravation beyond natural progression. When the answer is no, a denial is not the end: the appeal system exists because first decisions are often incomplete. The reasonable doubt rule (§ 4.3) means that when the evidence is in equipoise, the veteran wins. That is the law.

Where to get help

- Accredited VSOs (DAV, VFW, American Legion, and others) help veterans file and appeal claims at no cost. They are the first call for most veterans.

- Accredited attorneys and claims agents — for complex claims, especially secondary service connection and nexus disputes. Verify accreditation through the VA Office of General Counsel.

- Independent medical opinions. A well-reasoned IMO from a qualified clinician can supply or strengthen the nexus a claim requires. This is the work Monumental Health was founded to do — not to sell a conclusion, but to provide the evidence-integrated medical reasoning that a fragmented record often lacks.

- The Veterans Crisis Line (988, then press 1) exists alongside the claims process. Your life matters regardless of your rating.

What to take with you

1. A service-connected condition can cause or aggravate a new condition, and that secondary condition can itself be rated under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310(a). Under Adams, the SC condition need only be a cause, not the cause.

2. Aggravation is different from causation. Under Spicer and Allen, the compensable question is whether a condition was worsened beyond its natural progression — and the rating covers the degree of aggravation, not the full condition.

3. A denial is not the end of the line. The appeal system — Supplemental Claim, Higher-Level Review, Board Appeal — exists because first decisions are often incomplete, not because the veteran was wrong.

4. The reasonable doubt rule (§ 4.3) is the law. When evidence is in equipoise, the veteran wins.

5. The system is navigable, and you are not alone. Advocacy is not adversarial; it is the faithful translation of a life into the language the system was built to hear.

You now have the map, the vocabulary, and the recourse. Use them.
